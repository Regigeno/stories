*Saturday, 4:30 p.m.*

The Reptile House had been built in 1934 to hold snakes, and for the last two and a half hours it had held a dinosaur.

Imogen sat on an overturned bench in its long central hall with her back against a cracked terrarium, a dart rifle across her knees and her right arm pressed to her ribs, and listened to Atlas eat the ferns.

He was eating them slowly, with a great deal of care. The hall was a long arched space of green tile and brick, a hundred and fifty feet from the chained front doors to the iron stair at the back, lined on both sides with glass display cases that had been empty for two years. Someone, years ago, had planted the cases with tropical ferns for the sake of the frogs. The frogs were gone. The ferns had not noticed. They had grown out of their cases and through their broken glass and across the floor, and now a runt Brontosaurus, twenty feet from nose to tail, stood among them with his long neck bent and his head, no larger than a horse's, plucking the green fronds one by one with the delicate, deliberate lips of an animal that had been told, when it was very small, to eat nicely.

Above him, a banner hung in tatters from the arched ceiling. WELCOME TO THE REPTILE HOUSE! it said, over a cartoon of three smiling frogs. Half the roof had gone in the chiller fire, which had burned itself out on the copper before anyone could have seen the smoke. Through the hole, a ragged square of clean blue autumn sky looked down on her, tinged now with the first long gold of the afternoon.

"Good," she said, softly. "That's good. Eat."

He lifted his head and looked at her. There was a long smear of dried blood down his left flank, where Kofi's hooves had found him. There was a bruise the size of a dinner plate under it, purple going yellow at the edges. He moved on that side with a faint, careful stiffness that made her chest hurt. He had been kicked by a giraffe, in a paddock, in front of a crowd, by a creature a third his weight, and he had not understood any of it. He had only wanted to be somewhere that smelled of green.

She put out her hand. He lowered his head, slowly, and let her lay her palm flat on the bridge of his nose, a hand-width of warm, dry, rough skin, ridged like the bark of a young tree.

And there it was. The thing she had never been able to explain to anyone who had not felt it. A hum, very low, rising up out of him and into her palm, her wrist, the bones of her arm. It was not a sound. It was a vibration, as if she had put her hand on the hull of a great ship, and it went into her chest and found her own pulse and settled there.

He did it when he was content. He had done it since he was the size of a Labrador. She had once spent a whole night in the nursery with her palm on his side, feeling it, because she could not sleep and he did not mind.

"I know," she said. "I know. I'm sorry."

The ribs hurt, on the right. She had counted three that she was fairly sure were cracked, on the stairs, an hour ago. She had not had the heart to feel for a fourth.

On the floor beside her, Tamsin Ode's radio sat on the bench. It was a Channel Two handset in a rubber boot, scuffed, with a strip of green tape across the back that read ODE. She had picked it up from the grass at Giraffe Overlook two hours ago. She had not been able to put it down since.

She did not like to look at the radio, so she looked at the slate instead.

It was a Meridian field tablet, a ruggedized thing the size of a paperback, and it was the only piece of the old life she had carried up the stairs. On its screen, over a gray map of the zoo, four dots glowed in four colors. Green for J-1, which was a small, steady pulse at the center of the Reptile House, which was *him*. Red for R-1. Blue for S-1. Yellow for U-2.

She had been watching them for two and a half hours.

The red dot had not moved in forty minutes. It sat in the middle of the Carousel Court, in the very center of the plaza, a bright small point on the map, and each time she looked at it, she thought of the number the map did not show, which was the number of people who had been standing in the plaza when it arrived.

She did not know it. She had stopped trying to guess.

The blue dot had moved. Slowly, heavily, in a long uneven line, it had crawled out of the lagoon and across the lawns toward the center of the zoo, pausing for ten and fifteen minutes at a time. S-1 was hurt. She did not know how badly. She only knew that her last good look at the readout had shown a body temperature two degrees above normal and a heart rate that did not slow.

The yellow dot was underground. It moved in a long loop through the Ring, the zoo's old tunnels, back and forth along the same curve. It was hunting. She knew it was hunting, because it was what Jazz did, with her one working eye and her ruined shoulder. She did it for the sake of doing it.

And the floor, under her bench, hummed.

She felt it again. A long, slow pressure through the brick, through the soles of her shoes. A deep, patient beat, one and then a long gap and then another. It was a sound she had never heard. It was a sound she had felt, a hundred times, through glass, in Paddock Three, on her best days and her worst. She would have known it on the moon.

Duchess was calling.

Not to anyone. She was calling the way a person hums in an empty house. Low, sub-audible, a note too slow for the ear, rolling out over the hill in all directions. It meant: *here. I am here. Is anyone here?*

Atlas lifted his head from the ferns. His nostrils flared. The hum in his chest stopped. For a long moment, he stood perfectly still with a frond hanging out of his mouth, and she watched his small, patient eyes look past her to the broken wall and the sky. Then he turned his neck, and answered it.

She felt that, too.

It went through her like a struck bell. A single low note, no louder than a sigh, from a creature as large as a small bus, and she knew exactly what it was. It was the thing a child does at night when he hears a sound in the house, and calls out, softly, to the dark: *Mother?*

"No," Imogen said. "No, sweetheart. Don't. Not her."

He turned his head back to her. After a moment, he lowered it, and put it in her lap, an enormous, warm, heavy weight, with his chin on her knee and the rifle pushed aside.

She put both hands on his nose and closed her eyes.

* * *

The radio had been talking for a long while before she noticed.

It had been full at three o'clock. A dozen voices then, keepers calling positions, a man counting heads out loud, Bev's voice cracking on the Ops channel. By four the channel had thinned to static and a handful of names, and she had stopped keeping track of whose had stopped. It had been so long since she'd let herself listen that the sound seemed to arrive from the other side of a wall. But the voice on it was a voice she knew, and it was saying her name.

"...Dr. Vale. Imogen. Come in, please. Dr. Vale, this is Bao. Please."

She reached for the handset. Her hand shook, and she watched it do so with a distant professional interest.

"Bao," she said. "It's Imogen. I'm here."

"Thank God." His voice cracked on it, and for a second there was only the hiss of the channel. Then it came back, quick, quiet, a young man's voice trying to sound older than it was. "I've been calling for twenty minutes. I couldn't tell if you had Dr. Ode's radio or if somebody else did. Are you hurt?"

"Some ribs. I'm fine. Where is Dale?"

"Next to me. He's right here. He's, ah. He's hurt too. But he's all right." A pause. "We have the control room. Ruiz and the others are locked on Sublevel One with the investors. Teague isn't with them. I don't know where he is."

"He's below," Imogen said. "Sublevel Five. He's the only one with the key."

"What's on Sublevel Five?"

"I don't know."

There was a long quiet. She looked at the slate. The yellow dot had stopped moving. It sat at a junction in the Ring, a hundred yards from the elephant barn, and she watched it stay there.

"Doctor," Bao said. "Listen. I need to tell you something and you won't like it. I've been through the schedule. All the schedules. I went through every automated job on the system, because I thought, if I can find what Teague's going to use, I can shut it off. And I found it. It's the feed tone."

"The feed tone."

"Six o'clock. Every paddock. Every day." He drew a breath. "I thought it was just a bell. I put it on the wall because it was the only thing we never got wrong. But it's not on the B loop. It's got its own battery. It's wired straight to the paddock speakers and the Ring and the lift halls, and it doesn't go through any of the door controllers. I can't stop it. I looked. I tried for an hour. It's hardwired, Doctor. It's going to ring at six whether we want it to or not."

"All right," she said. "So it rings. What does it do?"

"I don't know yet. But I know what it did to them when they were small." His voice dropped. "You designed the feeding protocol. You know. They were hatched to it. Every one of them. Every day of their lives, six o'clock, they heard that bell, and then they were fed. And there's an infrasound layer, in the recording, under the bell. You put that in. Right?"

She did not answer.

She had put it in. It had been 2:00 a.m. in her second year. Her first Jurassic hatchling, small as a spaniel and half starved, had been refusing to eat, and she had lain on the nursery floor trying to think what a creature of that kind would want. She had remembered a paper on sauropod hearing. She had built a sound, a soft, low pulse under the bell, below the range of human ears, a thing an animal would feel in its sternum like a mother's heartbeat, and she had played it. And he had come across the floor to her and *eaten*.

It had been the happiest night of her working life.

"They'll come," Bao said. "Won't they? At six. Whatever they're doing, wherever they are. Duchess. The Spinosaur. Jazz. Atlas. They'll hear it in their bones, and they'll come home."

"Yes," Imogen said.

"Teague knows. He told Dale. He said, quite calmly, that all he has to do is wait. At six the assets recall themselves. He says we only have to be ready at the doors." Bao made a small, dry, awful sound that was almost a laugh. "He says it's a *managed outcome*."

"He said that."

"Dale didn't say anything. He just looked at me. I think he's thinking something, Doctor. I think he's got a plan. He won't tell me what yet."

She looked down at the huge gray head in her lap. His eyes had closed. His breathing was slow and heavy and even, a sound like a bellows in a quiet house.

*Six o'clock. They'll come.*

It was 4:51.

"Bao," she said. "Listen to me. I have something to ask, and I need you to say yes."

"Anything."

"The freight lift in the Reptile House. The big one, behind the iron stair. It's on its own circuit, right? It isn't on the B loop?"

"It's, yes. It's on C loop. It'll run. But the lift hall's sealed, it's an exterior door, under the site-seal. I'd have to open it from the console."

"Can you?"

"I can, if I'm quick. Ruiz can't hear me now. But Doctor, why?"

She was already moving. She eased Atlas's head from her lap, gently, an inch at a time, and set it on the folded jacket she had been using as a pillow. He grunted in his sleep. She got to her feet, and the pain in her ribs flared white and settled.

"Because I have to get to the control room before six," she said. "I designed the feeding system. I know where the amplifiers are, and I know every line of the code. If I can't turn it off, at least I can *change it.* I can alter what they hear. I can send them somewhere else."

"Where?"

"I don't know. Anywhere but here."

A pause. She could hear him breathing.

"It's sixty feet down the freight lift," Bao said. "Then a service corridor. Then Paddock Three, which, I should tell you, is where the loading dock is, and the Ring hatches. It's the quickest way in. It's also the quickest way for everything else to come in, at six."

"I know."

"Doctor, you'll have maybe forty minutes."

"Then I'd better hurry."

"And Atlas?"

She looked at him.

It was the question she had been not asking for two and a half hours. He lay on the brick floor under the ruined roof, in the ferns, in the long gold light, with his legs folded under him and his bruised side rising and falling. He was twenty feet of living history. He was the only Jurassic creature on earth, and his flesh held the only copy of a hundred and fifty million years of a code that no one would ever see again. If he died, it would die with him, and the work of her life, and the work of everyone before her, would not come back. She had known it from the moment she'd seen his dot on the map. She had known it on the stairs, with her ribs cracking. She had known it when she ran into a paddock with a giraffe, for his sake, with a dart gun and no plan. She had let a woman die for it. Tamsin Ode had said, "Imogen, get out of there," and Imogen had not got out, and then the tail had come round, and the rail, and Tamsin with it.

*You did not do that for the species.*

She did not know whom it was she had done it for.

"He stays here," she said. "In the main hall. The lift hall doors have a bar on my side. He'll be safe for an hour."

"Is he calm?"

"He's asleep."

"Doctor." Bao hesitated. "At six, if the tone rings and he's near the speakers, he won't be asleep."

"I know," Imogen said.

She knelt. She put her hand on the great gray flank, the warm one, and felt the hum come back, faint and slow, like a drowsy cat. She stayed there for a long moment with her cheek against his side.

Then she picked up the rifle and the slate and Tamsin Ode's radio, and she went down the long hall of ferns to the back of the building.

* * *

The freight lift was at the back of the building, behind a pair of steel doors thirty feet tall that had once been painted a cheerful green. Beyond them lay the lift hall, a high concrete room the size of a gymnasium with a yellow line painted around its floor and a freight cage in the middle of it, big enough to hold a bus.

She stood in the doorway and looked at it.

On the far wall, a speaker box hung from a bracket, a squat gray horn with a Meridian logo stencilled on the side. She had specified it herself, in an office, in another life. She had never in her life thought of it as a weapon.

"Bao. I'm at the lift."

"I see you on the camera. Lift hall's unlocking. Cage is coming up. Give it ninety seconds."

She turned back.

He was standing in the doorway behind her. She did not know how long he had been there. He had woken, and risen, and followed her down the long hall of ferns on his stiff, careful legs, and now he stood with his neck stretched out to her and his head cocked, and the long gold light behind him.

"No," she said. "Stay."

He did not stay. He came to her. He lowered his head and pressed it, very gently, against her chest, over the ribs, so that the pain went through her like a flash of light, and she did not step away. He hummed. She felt it all through her body, in her cracked ribs and her teeth and the soles of her feet.

*Don't leave.*

She held him with both arms. It was the first time since he was the size of a spaniel that she had been able to put both arms around him. She had touched him through the bars of a crate, and a plastic curtain, and the hatch of a paddock. She had not felt the whole warm weight of him in years.

"I'm coming back," Imogen said, to the dinosaur. "I promise. I'm coming back for you."

It was a lie, and she knew it. And she thought, with a cold clarity that she would remember for the rest of her life, that it was perhaps the first lie she had ever told that she wished with her whole soul she could make true.

Behind her the freight cage settled into place with a clang.

She took one step back. Atlas followed. She took another. He followed that too. At the steel doors of the lift hall, she stopped and put her hand flat on his nose, and then she did the hardest thing she had ever done, which was to step through and swing the doors shut between them.

The bar came down with a sound like a gunshot.

On the other side, there was a long, long silence. Then something large leaned against the doors, very softly, and the steel flexed by a hair, and a single deep note came up through the concrete and into her feet.

It was not a cry. It was too low for a cry. It was the sound a child makes when he is left in a dark room and does not yet understand that it is not a game.

Imogen Vale put her hand against the cold green steel, and held it there.

It was 4:58.

Somewhere above her, through the broken roof, a pair of crows went by, calling. Somewhere beyond the hill, a very long way off, a carousel had stopped playing. And sixty feet below, under the zoo, a laminated schedule hung on a control-room wall in a young man's tidy hand: *FEED TONE 18:00, ALL PADDOCKS.*
