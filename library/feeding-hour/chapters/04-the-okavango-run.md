*Saturday, 1:58 p.m.*

Theo Lindqvist felt it in the soles of his boots before the water told him anything.

He was standing at the wheel of the *Marjorie*, a twenty-eight-foot pontoon boat with a striped canopy and a seating capacity of twelve, halfway through the loop of the Okavango Lagoon with eleven passengers behind him and a microphone in his hand. For eleven years he had read weather off the deck of container ships. He had read a gale in the way a rail hummed. He knew the difference between an engine that was tired and an engine that was lying.

This was neither. This was a tremor, very slow and very low, coming up through the deck plates and the soles of his feet and the bones of his legs and settling into his chest like a second heartbeat. His coffee, in the cup holder by the throttle, drew a perfect circle of rings on its surface and held them.

"...and if you look to your left," he said into the microphone, because the job was the job, "you'll see Gideon and his family, our resident bloat of hippopotamuses. That's the correct term, folks. A bloat. Five of them. Gideon's the big fellow in the middle. He weighs about three tons and he's responsible for more deaths in Africa each year than lions, so please keep your hands in the boat."

A ripple of laughter behind him. A man's voice: "Is that true?"

"Every word," Theo said. "Their favorite hobby is being furious."

He looked left. Gideon's bay lay a hundred yards off, a wide, shallow curve of green water with a muddy beach at one end, and the five hippos were in it, as they always were at this hour, shoulder to shoulder in the shallows, mostly submerged, with their eyes and ears and nostrils above the surface like five small lumpy islands. There was a mother with a young one. There was the big bull, with his scarred, gray, enormous head. Sometimes, in the afternoons, the whole group would crack their mouths open in a yawn that would stop a tourist's heart.

They were not yawning. They were not doing anything.

All five of them, together, had turned their heads to the north end of the lagoon and gone completely still.

Theo had worked around animals only a little in his life. But there was a thing he had learned on ships, which was that the creatures on a vessel always knew a storm before the instruments did. Rats left. Gulls stopped. And that stillness was the same here, the same flat, listening stillness, as if the whole lagoon had been told to hold its breath.

His radio crackled. It was a handheld clipped to his belt, a Channel Two unit that all the zoo's staff carried, and in three years at the zoo he had never learned to like the way it sat on his hip like a pistol.

"Any boat, any boat, any staff on the water," said a woman's voice, high and quick. It was Bev, from Ops. "This is Ops. We have a, we have a situation. Gates are locked. All visitors back to the center, please, as fast as you can do it safely. Repeat, all staff bring your visitors to the center. I don't know why. I don't know. Just do it."

"Marjorie copies," Theo said into the handheld. "Coming about. Five minutes to the pier."

"Mr. Lindqvist? What's going on?" It was the woman in the front row, in a puffy blue coat with her teenage daughter next to her, a tall girl of fifteen or so with her hair in two braids and a phone held out over the water. She had a flat, sensible voice, and she had not been laughing at the jokes.

"A staff announcement, ma'am. Nothing to worry about. We'll head in a little early." He eased the throttle forward and brought the wheel over. "Folks, if you could keep seated, please."

He did not say what else he was thinking. He was thinking that no man at a ticket desk had ever told him that the gates had *locked*.

"My phone's dead," the girl said, to nobody. "I've got no bars. It was fine two minutes ago."

"Jess," said her mother, mildly.

"Mom. Look at it."

The woman looked. She had a way of looking at things that Theo recognized. She had looked at the ripple in his coffee, and at the hippos, and at the sky. She was somebody who had spent time in rooms where something was going wrong.

"Mr. Lindqvist," she said quietly, so the children in the back wouldn't hear. "I'm a paramedic. Rosalind Pike. If you need a hand with anything, I'm here."

"Thank you, Ms. Pike," Theo said. "Let's hope I don't."

He swung the *Marjorie* around in a slow arc. The pier lay at the south end, a long wooden finger with a ticket hut. The boat's twin outboards muttered under him. He felt the tremor in the hull again, stronger, and this time it carried a sound with it, so low that for a second he thought it was the engine hitting a bad note. It was not a noise a person's ears could hold. It was a pressure, a beat like a distant bass drum, slow and heavy and patient, moving through the water and up through the pontoons and into the seat of his chest.

*Thud.* And a long space. *Thud.*

A child in the back said, "Dad, the water's *shaking.*"

It was. All across the surface, in the bay, along the shore, out toward the island of dead trees in the middle, a million tiny rings had sprung up at once, a skin of shivering ripples that ran from the north end of the lagoon like frost across a window.

Theo looked north.

There was a drain at the north end. He knew it well, since the maintenance crew drained the lagoon every spring through a concrete culvert in the bank, a round black mouth twelve feet across, grated over, with a trickle of water that ran out of it day and night. The grate was a lattice of steel bars as thick as his wrist. He had cleaned leaves from it with a pole.

The grate was gone.

It was not broken. It was gone. It lay on the shore ten yards from the opening, flung there, bent double like a paper clip, and the black mouth of the culvert behind it had begun to pour out a thick brown rush of water, a slow wave rolling out into the clear green lagoon.

Something was in it.

"Everybody sit down," Theo said, quietly, and surprised himself with how steady it came out. "Sit on the benches. Keep your hands in the boat. Ms. Pike, I'd take it as a favor if you'd count heads."

"Eleven," she said at once. "Plus you."

"Good."

He opened the throttle all the way. The *Marjorie* was not built for speed. She lifted her nose, wallowed, and began to creep toward the pier at nine knots, shaking in her seams.

On the north shore, the crocodiles began to move.

There were eight of them in the exhibit, Nile crocodiles, and Theo knew each by sight. They were not friends. In the wild, in Africa, they would have shared a river without a word. Here, in a space the size of a city block, they had divided the shoreline into eight kingdoms, and each had its own mudbank and its own log, and each stayed on its own, and they passed one another in the water without a glance, the way strangers did in an elevator. He had once watched one of them, the biggest, an old male the keepers called Old Nile, a fifteen-foot battleship of a thing with half a tail missing, lie on his bank for six hours without moving a scale.

Old Nile was moving now. He had come off his bank in a long, flat rush and slid into the water, and he was *swimming away*, south, toward the boat, with his whole body working and his head high. To his left, a smaller croc on a log slid into the lagoon. To his right, another. One by one, up and down the shore, the eight kingdoms were emptying. Each went on its own, with no coordination, no pack, no plan. They simply left, each on its own heading and each as fast as it could, the way a room full of strangers leaves a burning building.

"Why are the crocodiles swimming at us?" said a boy.

"They're not swimming at us, buddy," Theo said. "They're swimming *away*."

The water at the mouth of the culvert bulged.

It rose in a long dome, the size of a delivery truck, and stood there, and parted. Something came up through it, and it was dark, and it was ridged, and it was long. It was a fin. It was a sail, a great thin curved sail of skin stretched over a row of spines, six feet tall and as wide as a barn door, the color of a bruise. It broke the surface, and water ran off the ridged membrane in sheets. Behind it a long gray back rose, and behind that, a second ripple, which was the beginning of a tail.

"What is that," said Rosalind Pike. She said it in a low flat voice with no question in it.

Theo did not answer. He was looking at the head.

It rose out of the brown water at the culvert mouth the way a periscope rises, on a thick neck, pale and scaly, with a long, narrow, crocodile-shaped snout lined with a double row of straight, conical teeth. The nostrils sat high on the skull, far back, so it could breathe while it swam. A small crest of bone stood between the eyes like a sail's smaller sibling. The eyes were dark, and they were set to the sides, and the head swung slowly across the lagoon from left to right and left again, searching.

It was longer than the boat. It was longer than the *Marjorie* and the pier together. As it lifted its shoulders from the water, he saw the arms, long and thick and clawed, hanging in front of its chest like those of a bear, and the whole body, from the nose to the base of the tail, went on and on in a smooth, unbroken curve that seemed too long to belong to anything alive.

Everything went quiet.

No gulls. No carousel. Not even the little engines of the *Marjorie* seemed to be making a sound. It was a silence of pure attention.

And then, from somewhere in that long gray chest, the note came again.

Theo felt it before he heard it. It struck the hull from beneath, and the pontoons rang like a pair of struck bells, and the coffee in his cup leapt out of the cup and splashed his wrist. In the back of the boat a woman cried out. The pressure was in his teeth, and in his eyes, and in the fillings of his jaw. It was a sound too big for the air, an impossibly deep, impossibly slow *boom*, and it rolled out over the lagoon and met the far bank and came back as a faint echo.

The hippos answered it.

Not with sound. They *stood*. All five, together, lifted themselves out of the shallows in a slow upwelling of mud and water, and for the first time Theo saw how enormous they really were. Gideon stood in the middle with his great head lowered, his jaw hinged half open, his bared tusks the length of a man's forearm. His small round ears were flat. Behind him, the cows pressed shoulder to shoulder, with the young one tucked behind the matriarch's flank. They did not run. They stood with their feet planted and watched it.

"Theo," said Rosalind, softly. "Maybe we go faster."

"The throttle's open."

"Then maybe a bit more open."

The animal in the water made a decision.

He saw it happen. The head swung away from the hippos, and away from the boat, and toward the shore, to the north bank, where one of the crocodiles, a young female, small and fast, still lay on her mud, ten yards from the water and hesitating. The spinosaur's neck went low. The sail dipped. The great body tilted and slid forward without a ripple, quick as a snake, and in three strides of that long tail, it was *there*.

He saw the jaws open. He saw the teeth, a double row of them, straight and slender as a hundred knives. He did not see the rest. A hand had closed over his arm, and it was Rosalind's, and she had put herself in front of the girl, and she said, in her flat voice, "Don't look, honey," and Jess said, "Mom, I'm not a *baby,*" in a voice that cracked on the last word.

There was a sound, small and wet, like a branch snapping underwater.

Then silence.

"One," Theo said, aloud, without meaning to.

He had never said it before. It was the sort of thing a man said on a ship when a wave took a crate off the deck and he wanted to know how many were left. His hands were steady on the wheel. They were always steady. That was the thing about him, the thing his ex-wife had hated and his sponsor had praised: under pressure his hands went still. They had been still the night he lost his license, and still on the morning he stopped drinking, and they were still now.

*Marjorie* crawled south through the shivering water, nine knots, eight, nine.

The pier was two hundred yards off. He could see the ticket hut. He could see a pair of keepers in green jackets standing at the end of the dock, waving, and behind them a family running down the boardwalk, a mother pulling a small child by the arm. They were waving the boat in, and they could not know what was behind it, and he wanted to shout to them to run.

"There's another one," said Rosalind.

On the east shore, a crocodile was running. Not swimming. *Running*, up the muddy beach on four short, bent legs, with its belly lifted clear of the ground, toward a stand of reeds. It was Old Nile. He was the oldest creature in the lagoon, with fifteen feet of scar and armor, and he was fleeing across dry land in a gallop that Theo had not known a crocodile could do.

The spinosaur lifted its head from its first kill, and its eyes found him.

It came across the lagoon in a long, rolling glide. It did not hurry. It did not need to. Its legs, which on land had looked short beside its immense body, drove it through the water with long, powerful sweeps, and the great paddle of its tail lashed side to side behind it, and the sail cut the surface like the dorsal fin of a whale. In ten seconds it had covered half the lagoon.

It was not coming for the boat.

It was coming for Old Nile.

"Hold on," Theo said. "All of you. Hold on to something."

He did not know why he said it. He had no reason to think that anything was coming for the boat. But his hands, steady on the wheel, had already put the helm over, and the *Marjorie*, wallowing, had already begun to swing out of the line between the animal and its prey, and by luck or by instinct, or by eleven years of reading the sea, he had put the pontoon boat exactly where he should not have.

The spinosaur cut across their bow.

It was a distance of perhaps thirty feet. Theo would think about that later. Thirty feet, no more, a pale gray shoulder rising out of the lagoon like the flank of a whale, the water pouring off it in sheets, the thick ridged sail standing level with the canopy, with a wet slap of membrane. The pontoons shuddered as the wake hit them. A wave came over the bow and broke across the deck, and Theo's boots were suddenly ankle-deep in cold green water, and somewhere in the back a child began to scream.

The great head turned, as it passed, and looked at the boat.

It was a bird's look. It was the cool, curious, sidelong look of a heron regarding a minnow. There was no malice in it. There was no hunger either, at least not for them. There was only a calm, huge, utterly indifferent attention, as if the animal were weighing a thing that might be food and might be an obstacle, and had not yet decided which.

Then the tail came round.

He saw it start. It swung out of the water behind the body, thick as an oak, a flattened, paddle-shaped, ridged thing the size of a truck door, and he understood in the same instant what it was going to hit and that there was nothing on earth he could do about it.

"Down!" Theo shouted. "Everybody *down!*"

The tail struck the *Marjorie* amidships.

It was not a violent blow. That was the horror of it. It was almost casual, the sweep of a limb that did not know it was striking anything, and the whole boat lifted out of the water, sideways, canopy and benches and eleven screaming people, and hung for a moment in the air with the lagoon spinning below her. Theo saw the sky. He saw the white clouds, and the carousel's distant roof, and a flock of startled pigeons going up from the viewing deck. He saw the girl, Jess, tumbling over the rail with her braids flying.

Then the water came up and took them all.
