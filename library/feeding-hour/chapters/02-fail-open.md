*Saturday, 1:10 p.m.*

Through the gallery glass, forty feet below, the Tyrannosaurus was asleep, and Imogen Vale was the only person in the room who knew that was a bad sign.

"She looks peaceful," said the man from the investment fund, whose name was Carrow, and who had been saying things like that for twenty minutes. He stood with his hands behind his back in a suit that cost more than Imogen's first car, and he leaned toward the glass the way people leaned toward the edge of a roof. "I thought she'd be, you know. Angrier."

"She's not angry," Imogen said. "She's conserving energy."

"For what?"

"For whatever happens next. That's all any of them do. Rest, wait, and then spend everything at once."

Beside Carrow, the woman from the other fund, Whitcomb, did not look at the dinosaur. She looked at Imogen, and Imogen recognized the look. It was the one she gave her own graduate students when they said a result was fine and their hands said otherwise.

Paddock Three was the largest room beneath Calder Bay. It had been a rail repair shed once, a hundred and twenty years ago, and then a basement, and then a hole in the ground, and Meridian Biologics had poured nine thousand cubic yards of concrete into it and painted the result to look like a river valley. Cypress trunks stood in a pretend swamp. Sodium lamps burned in the ceiling at the color of a late afternoon that never came. In the middle of it, in a bed of ferns trampled flat, lay R-1, whom the staff called Duchess, forty feet from snout to tail, eight tons of her, folded up on her own legs like a vast and patient bird.

The feathers were the part Imogen could never get the investors to believe. Not on the adults, not really. A thin gray fuzz along the spine and the back of the neck, a scruff like the down on a hawk, and that was all. But under the lamps it caught the light, and it moved when she breathed, and it was the single detail that made people stop looking at her as a movie and start looking at her as an animal.

An animal that had never seen the sky.

"Dr. Vale." Warren Teague's voice came softly from behind her, at the exact volume he used when he was about to ask for something. "A word, when you have a moment? Not about the tour."

"I always have a moment, Warren."

"I know. It's one of the things I admire."

He was a tall man in his early fifties with a calm, handsome face and gray at the temples that she had once suspected was styled. He had never raised his voice in the eleven years she had known him. He held the Meridian Biologics badge number 0001 and a lift key that no one else was allowed to touch, and he had a way of saying *we* that made her feel, for a quarter of a second, like she had been given something.

She followed him out of the gallery and into the corridor.

"The demonstration," Teague said. "Phase Two. The pack feeding at two-fifteen. Carrow's committee wants to see the Velociraptors take live prey. They don't want a video. They want to watch it."

"Warren, we discussed this. I said not today."

"You said you had concerns about the cooling plant."

"I said the cooling plant is two weeks old and I have never seen a new system put through its first full-load day on a Saturday with every pump running and a hundred and forty people in the building." She kept her voice level. "Bao has been flagging the chiller output since eleven. It's running hot. The load on the B loop is higher than the model."

"And Bao is an excellent engineer. I've asked him to monitor it."

"And the door controllers are on the B loop."

"They are."

"So if the B loop fails, the controllers fail."

"And revert to their default state, which we've discussed," Teague said, patiently, "and which is not the end of the world, because the hatches are sealed from the zoo side and every animal is inside a locked paddock."

"Which is why the default state needs to be closed."

"Imogen."

He let the word sit. She knew the rest of this conversation. They had had it in March, when Bao Tran had walked into Teague's office with a forty-page report titled *Request for Change 114: Fail-Closed Door Logic*, and walked out of it twenty minutes later with a promise that it would be handled in Phase Two. In May, when the new chiller was approved. In September, when it was installed. Each time, she had been in the room. Each time, she had said she was concerned, and each time she had signed the next page, because Phase Two was coming, because the funding was real, because the project was the only thing in her life that had ever worked.

"Fail-open," Teague said, gently, "was a design choice. We made it so that, in a fire, the animals could never be trapped in a burning room. You helped write that rationale."

"I wrote it for fires."

"A power failure is a fire. In the abstract." He smiled, to show her it was a joke, which was how she knew he meant it. "I'm not dismissing you. If the chiller trips, we'll shed load, we'll switch to backup, we'll adjust. Calloway's team is on every door. We're a very prepared company."

She looked at him for a long moment. She could hear, behind the wall, the great slow bellows of the air handlers, and under it, so faint that you stopped hearing it after your first week, a high bright tinkling sound that was not part of the machinery at all.

It was the carousel. Sixty feet overhead, through the ventilation shafts, the zoo was playing its music.

"It's a Saturday, Warren," she said. "There are people up there."

"There are always people up there. That's the point of the location." He touched her elbow. "Two-fifteen. Please."

* * *

The nursery wing lay on the far side of the sublevel, a long white corridor of paddocks and brooders, where the quiet felt more like a hospital than a lab. Imogen walked the length of it because she had told herself she needed to check the incubation logs, and because she did not trust herself to sit still.

At the end of the hall, a window looked into a space like a half-sized gymnasium, with a pool at one end and a heap of cut ferns at the other. In the corner of that room, sitting on his haunches like an enormous patient dog, was a young Brontosaurus.

He was thirty feet long now, nose to tail. He was five tons. He stood up when he saw her.

"Hello, Atlas," Imogen said, and put her palm flat against the glass.

J-1 came across the floor in four unhurried steps and lowered his long neck until his head, which was about the size of a horse's, hung level with her hand. A soft sound rumbled in his chest. His nostrils flared and fluttered, tasting her through the glass. She had never known whether he recognized her as a person or as a smell, a warm and familiar shape in the dark, but over the three years of his life it had stopped mattering.

He was the first. That was the part no one outside the sublevels would ever know. Before Duchess, before the Velociraptors, before the Spinosaur and the long, slow disaster of the Utahraptor, there had been a sauropod from the Late Jurassic, a hundred and fifty million years old, grown from a scrap of protein and a mountain of mathematics, and he had opened his eyes in a tank on a Tuesday morning and *looked at her*. The program had been about the Cretaceous ever since, a mandate written into the funding, and it was simply an administrative fact that the first animal had come from the wrong age. He was not supposed to exist on the books. He lived behind a door that said STORAGE.

"Nobody asked you to be the best thing I ever made," she told him. "That's not fair to you."

He blinked, slowly, a lid like a damp leaf.

"Dr. Vale." Dale Calloway's voice, behind her. She hadn't heard him come down the hall. He was a broad man, forty-four, with a retired soldier's neck and the faintly pained expression of someone who had been trained to be certain and employed to be quiet. "Control room's asking for you. Bao says it's urgent."

"How urgent?"

"He used the word *please*." Calloway's eyes moved to the window and the long gray head pressed to the other side of it. "He doesn't say please."

"What do you think, Dale?" She did not take her hand off the glass. "Do you think we're ready for this?"

He took his time. "I think," he said finally, "I can hear the music from here."

"So can I."

"There's a kid's birthday party on the lawn right above Paddock Two. I saw the balloons this morning on the camera." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I'm not paid to think about that. I'm paid to keep the doors shut."

"Are they shut?"

"They're shut," Calloway said, and neither of them believed that it was an answer.

* * *

The control room was a dim half-circle of monitors on Sublevel Three, with a window that looked out across the paddock floor and a raised bank of consoles in front of it. Bao Tran sat at the center with his sleeves rolled and two cold coffees at his elbow. He was thirty-one. He had not slept well in a month.

"Chiller Two is at a hundred and nine percent of rated capacity," he said, without turning. "It's been over a hundred since one-thirty. The B loop is pulling eleven percent more than I modeled. It's the pumps. I think the pumps are fighting each other."

"Can you throttle them?"

"I can throttle them. Then the paddock temps climb. Duchess goes from fourteen degrees to nineteen in about forty minutes, which she'll hate, and which will wake her up."

"And if you don't?"

"Then the loop trips." He finally looked at her, and his face was a very young face. "Doctor. I put it in writing. Four times."

"I know you did."

"Doctor Vale, I put the number on a sticky note on my monitor so I'd remember to ask you again. It says *114*." He tapped it. A square of yellow paper, curling at the edges. "I'm going to say something and I'd like you not to repeat it."

"Go ahead."

"I'm really scared of this building," Bao said.

Her mouth opened to answer. It was 1:51.

On the wall, above the paddock window, a row of green indicator lights blinked, all together, to amber.

"That's the B loop," said Bao, very softly.

And then, with a sound like someone hitting a switchboard with a hammer, the light in the room went out.

It came back almost at once, red this time, and the world was a dim crimson aquarium. An alarm began. Not one alarm but a layered chorus of them, high and low, climbing over each other. Screens snapped on and off. Overhead, a pair of ventilation fans spun down with a long, falling whine, and in the sudden quiet beneath it Imogen could hear her own pulse.

"Chiller Two tripped," Bao said, fast and clear. "Load-shed is cascading. It's dropping the B loop. It's dropping the controllers. Doctor, it's dropping the door controllers. And the shared feeder. The whole north hill is on our feeder, Taiga, the Reptile House, all of it. They're going dark up there too."

"Switch to backup."

"There's no backup for the doors. The backup *is* the B loop. It was in the Phase Two budget—"

"Then hold them manually."

"I can't hold what's gone."

The windows had turned black. On the center monitor, a map of the sublevel glowed in green and amber, row after row of door icons, each one a small padlock. As she watched, the padlocks began to open.

They did not open all at once. They opened in a ripple, as if something were counting them off. Paddock Seven. Paddock Five. The brooder annex. A pair of gates on the east wall. Then the long gray line of hatches along the north corridor that led out of the sublevel and into the Ring, the zoo's old service tunnels, and one by one the small green padlocks turned white.

"No," Bao whispered. "No, no. Come on."

"Calloway!" Imogen said.

"On it." He already had his radio at his mouth. "All teams, all teams, doors have failed open. I repeat, we've lost door control, all teams to your posts. Nobody enters a paddock. I say again, nobody enters a paddock."

"Dr. Vale," said a new voice from the back of the room.

Teague was standing in the doorway. She had not heard him arrive. His tie was straight. His hands hung at his sides. The red light turned his face into a mask and did not change the expression on it at all.

"How many?" he said.

"All of them," Bao said. "Everything in the north wing. Everything that has a hatch."

"Duchess?"

Bao touched a screen. A grainy gray image opened, the interior of Paddock Three, the cypress, the plastic swamp, the heap of trampled ferns. The ferns were empty. A pair of shadows moved through the lamp glare at the edge of the frame, and at the far end of the room, where the southern wall was cut by a black rectangle forty feet wide, something very large was just disappearing through it.

"She's in the Ring," Imogen heard herself say. "She's in the tunnel."

"The tunnel goes where?" Teague asked.

"You know where it goes."

"Humor me."

She turned. "It goes everywhere, Warren. It goes under the whole zoo. There are nine night houses off the Ring, one for every major exhibit. The zoo thinks they're sealed from the inside. They are *sealed from the zoo side*. The elephant barn is on that line. The tiger house. The lagoon pump station. They've been bolted from their side for two years and nobody knows what's on the other end of the bolt."

"Then the animals will be contained by the night houses."

"A Tyrannosaurus can't be contained by a barn door."

"I'm aware."

"Then call it in." She was shouting now. She could not remember deciding to. "Call the zoo. Call the director. Call the police. We need an evacuation. We need the gates open, we need every person out of that park, we need it right now."

"We do," said Teague.

For one moment she felt a bright and ridiculous hope.

"We need to contain the problem," he went on, in the same calm tone, "before anyone upstairs knows it exists." He lifted his wrist and spoke into it. "Calloway, this is Teague. Initiate site-seal. Full protocol. Lock all exterior doors, engage the exterior jammers, and activate remote lock on the zoo's front and service gates."

"And Calloway," Teague added, to the room, "have someone sit with our guests in the Sublevel One lounge. Keep them comfortable. Keep them away from windows."

Calloway had lowered his radio. He stood between the consoles with his mouth slightly open.

"Sir," he said. "The gates. Sir, there's two thousand people in there."

"The gates stay shut, Dale. The animals stay inside the fences. The fences are perfectly good fences. We will have the sublevel secured in twenty minutes and we will have the animals back by dinner."

"Warren." Imogen found that she had taken a step toward him. "Warren, those fences were built for lions."

"Then they'll hold something."

"You're locking the *visitors in*."

"I'm locking the *problem* in." He looked at her at last, and there was something in his calm that she had not seen before, and that she realized, with a long cold feeling in her stomach, that she had been mistaking for kindness for eleven years. It was only certainty. "If those gates open, Imogen, every phone in that park films a dinosaur. If those gates open, the program ends. The funding ends. Atlas ends. Everything you have built ends in an afternoon, and for what? A panicked crowd, a stampede. People die in stampedes. This way, the damage is contained. This way, we manage it."

"People will die."

"People die at zoos," Teague said. "It's not a pleasant truth. But it is a truth."

On the console behind her, a white-on-black message blinked into being: SITE-SEAL ENGAGED. Overhead, with a heavy, final thud, a pair of steel shutters came down over the control-room door. She heard the lock catch.

Bao made a small choked sound. He had grabbed his desk phone and put it to his ear. He took it down. He looked at it.

"Line's dead," he said. "Cell's dead. The jammer's up. It's up on every channel."

"Radio?" said Imogen.

"Only inside the building. Only on the lab channel."

"Then we are going to get a message to the zoo another way." She turned to Calloway. "Dale. You have a key to the east stair."

"I do."

"The east stair leads up to the keepers' tunnel."

"It does." He looked at Teague, and then at her. His jaw moved, and she watched something in his face come loose, and it was not a quiet thing. "Sir. I'm taking Dr. Vale to the east stair."

"You are not," Teague said.

"With respect." Calloway drew a breath. "I swore an oath to a lot of things, and none of them was a company."

"I'll have you arrested."

"By who?" Calloway said. "The police are on the other side of a jammer you just turned on."

On the center monitor, Camera Ring-14 showed a long concrete tunnel lit in sodium orange. At the far end of it, striding unhurriedly toward a bolted steel hatch with a green stencil that read ELEPHANT HOUSE: NIGHT ACCESS, was something forty feet long, with a thin gray fuzz along its back, that stopped, and lowered its great head, and put its nose to the door.

The time stamp in the corner of the screen read 1:55.

Imogen Vale looked at it for a long moment. Somewhere far overhead, sixty feet up through the ventilation shafts, thin and bright and horribly cheerful, a carousel went on playing its tune.

"Go," she said.
