*Saturday, 1:54 p.m.*

The first thing Mags Delacroix noticed was that the sweet potatoes were walking.

They were stacked in a crate by the feed-room door, a hundred pounds of them, and one by one they were rolling gently toward the edge of the lid. She watched a big orange one reach the lip, hesitate, and drop to the concrete with a soft thud. It rolled in a slow arc and stopped against her boot.

"Well," she said to it. "That's new."

She had been at Calder Bay Zoo for thirty years, six months, and eleven days. She had cleaned up after the elephants through three directors, two hurricanes, one flood, and a goose that had taken a dislike to her in 1998 and never relented. She had seen the building shudder when the big trucks came by on Harbor Road. She had never seen it shiver like this, evenly, as if someone had set a hand on the whole barn and begun to hum.

Her left knee told her about it first, a dull ache from the floor up, like a tooth. Then the lights flickered. Once, twice, a long amber stutter, and came back on.

"Gerry," she called. "You feel that?"

"I do," said a voice from the doorway.

Gerry Ames was seventy-three, narrow as a fence post, in a Calder Bay Zoo volunteer vest that hung off his shoulders like a bedsheet. He had a clicker in one hand, the little steel kind that people used to count heads, and he was using it for no reason Mags could think of, click, click, click, as he looked down at the floor between his shoes. He had been a signalman on the railroad for forty years, and then the railroad had closed, and he had come to the zoo, which was built on the railroad's old yard, because, he said, he liked to keep an eye on the ground he used to work.

"It's the turntable," he said.

"The what?"

"The old roundhouse turntable. Sits right under us. This barn's on the pit." He nodded at the concrete. "They filled most of it in, back in the nineties. Poured a slab. But there's a cut, a service tunnel, runs under the whole hill. I used to walk it with my lantern when I was twenty. It's why this site's got all those old pipes." He frowned. "Something's moving in it."

"A truck."

"Not a truck."

"The new plumbing."

"Mags," Gerry said, mildly, "I was a signalman for forty years. I know what a train sounds like when it's coming. It sounds like that, only slower."

She looked at him. He was the sort of man you forgot you were standing next to. He had arrived in March with a lunchbox and a laminated badge, and by April, everybody knew him, and nobody could have said what he did. He checked the water troughs. He helped lost children find their parents. He had once, in July, quietly rerouted a whole school group around a ruptured pipe before the keepers had noticed the pipe was ruptured. He did it all without a word.

"Go and tell Carla to bring the visitors back from the rail," Mags said.

"All of them?"

"All of them. Say it's a drill. A stuck-door drill." She did not know why she'd said it. It sounded almost sane. "Tell her I said so."

He nodded once and went, and the clicker went with him, click, click, click, across the yard.

Mags stood in the barn, in the warm, grassy, familiar smell of hay and elephant, and listened.

Outside, in the sunshine, she could hear the voices of the afternoon crowd along the rail. A child laughed. A stroller wheel squeaked. Somewhere a woman called, "Elijah, stay with me." That sweet, ordinary, murmuring noise. It had been a good day. She had done a talk at one-thirty and the kids had asked sharp questions. A little girl had wanted to know if elephants cried, and Mags had said, "Sometimes. Not like us." And the little girl had said, "Do they know when *you* cry?" and Mags had said, "I couldn't say," and had gone behind the barn for a minute.

She had never told anyone what Imani did after Leon died. Four years in March. Leon, who had sat on a bucket outside the barn every Friday with a thermos of bad coffee and told Imani the news. Imani, who had stopped eating the week he died and who, on the ninth day, had walked to the fence where he used to sit, and put her trunk through the rails, and stood there, breathing out.

Mags did not believe in much. She believed in that.

The clock above the feed-room door said 1:56.

"Radio," she muttered, and thumbed the handset on her shoulder. "Ops, this is Mags at Plains. You there, Bev?"

Static. Then, a long moment later, a voice, tinny and uncertain. "Mags. Mags, thank God. I've been trying everybody."

"What's going on?"

"I don't know. The turnstiles just locked. Every one. Whole main gate, bars down like it's the end of the day. The phones are out. Cell's out too. Hugh's gone to look at the panel and I can't get anything on the office line. It just rings and rings."

"Locked how?"

"From the outside, he says. Some remote thing. We never had a remote thing." Bev's voice was climbing. "I've got two thousand people in here and I can't open a door."

"Two *thousand*?"

"It's the festival, Mags. It's a Saturday."

The radio crackled. Mags looked at the floor, where the sweet potato had begun, once again, to roll.

"Bev. Listen to me. I want you to call everybody on Channel Two. Every keeper. Tell them to bring their visitors to the center and keep them there. I'll explain when I know."

"Explain what?"

"I'll explain," Mags said, "when I know."

It was then that she heard it.

Not a roar, nothing so grand. It was a sound from the back wall. From the far end of the feed room, behind the pallets of hay and the stacked bags of pellets, where the plumbers' door had been bolted shut since before she was hired. A steel door, painted gray, in a recess nobody ever used. She had always assumed it went to a pipe room.

*Knock.*

A slow, heavy knock. Metal on metal. It echoed in the building like a bell in a well.

*Knock.*

Mags did not move.

The third knock was louder. And then the knocks stopped, and a different sound began, a long, rising shriek of tearing steel, the sound of a giant tin can being opened. She saw the pallets tremble. A stack of hay bales slid sideways and collapsed. Dust sifted from the rafters in a long gold curtain, and a handful of pigeons broke from the beams and fled through the open doorway into the afternoon.

She was behind the produce crates before she had decided to be.

It was the best hiding place in the room, which was not saying much: a corner between the cinderblock wall and a tall rolling cart of carrots and kale, with a gap between the crates just wide enough for one eye. She pressed her back against the block. Her heart was banging so hard that she thought it must be audible.

The door at the back of the feed room did not open. It *left*.

The bolts popped like gunshots. The whole door peeled off its frame and flew thirty feet across the barn and rang against the opposite wall, and where it had been, there was a rectangle of hot, wet, orange dark. A smell came out of it. Mags had smelled death before, a dozen dead animals, the kind of death that sits on a keeper's skin. This was not that. This was an old smell. Wet iron and swamp and something sweet and wrong underneath, like a pantry of meat left in the sun.

Then, through the doorway, a head.

It was too big to be a head. That was her first thought, and she held onto it, because it was easier than the second. It was a head the size of a refrigerator, long and wedge-shaped and scarred, gray at the muzzle, with a ridge of bone like a frowning brow over each eye. Fine gray feathers, nothing like the pictures, spiky down along the neck, like the fur of a wet cat. The mouth was closed. It was closed over teeth, and she could still see them: a row of dark, curved, serrated bananas, each as long as her hand, shining with saliva.

The head turned slowly, left, right, and it was *quiet*.

That was what she would remember afterward. It was eight tons of animal and it was nearly silent. It put its feet down like a cat, one at a time, toes first, as though the ground might give way, and the only sounds in all that room were the small dry tick of claws on concrete and the long, slow, wet *draw* of its breath.

It was smelling the barn.

Mags put her hand over her mouth. Her palm was damp.

The creature came into the feed room, and the room grew smaller. Its shoulders brushed the rafters. A beam splintered and fell. It put its head down, and nosed at a hay bale, and with a flick of its skull sent the bale spinning across the floor. Then, with an almost delicate interest, it lowered its jaws to the produce crates, and *sniffed*.

Carrots. Kale. A sweet potato.

It did not eat them. It was not interested in them. A nostril the size of a fist flexed and flared, and the whole head lifted, and swung toward the corner where Mags was.

She did not breathe.

The eye was the worst of it. It was not a monster's eye, nothing red or glowing. It was an amber disc the size of a saucer with a black pupil in the center, and the pupil was *contracting*. It was reading the dark. She watched it change, saw it narrow and focus, saw it look at the gap between the crates, and she understood with a total, animal clarity that she was not being seen.

She was being *sorted*.

Not a person. Not a threat. Not a *thing it was afraid of.* She was a warm shape, in a small place, of a size that fit. The head came down. The breath came across the crates in a hot gust, and the kale leaves rattled, and a thread of saliva as thick as a rope swung from its lower jaw and fell with a wet slap to the floor, a foot from her boot.

Mags's knee, her bad left knee, her treacherous old knee, chose that moment to *crack*.

It was a tiny sound. A pop, like a knuckle.

The head stopped moving.

The pupil went wide.

The thing lowered itself, shoulders rolling down and hips lifting, in a motion so smooth and so unmistakable that Mags's body knew it before her mind did. It was the posture of a cat at a mousehole. The mouth opened a few inches. A ribbon of steam rose from the dark back of its throat.

She thought, with perfect calm: *Oh. I'm dead.*

And then, from outside, through the open sliding door, over the sunshine and the dust, from forty yards away, a sound came that she had known for thirty years.

It was a trumpet.

It was not a scream. Imani never screamed. It was a long, rolling, brass-bright blast with a hard edge at the end of it, the sound she made when Pip wandered too far or a dog got into the yard. It was a summons. It was a challenge. It was a mother's voice, and it filled the barn from end to end.

The creature's head snapped round.

For a moment it was perfectly still. Then the great gray body unfolded and swung away from the crates, and Mags saw the whole length of it, the shoulders like a hill, the stunted arms folded against the chest, the immense tail swinging through the rafters and bringing down a rain of dust and tile. It moved toward the big sliding door, and the floor flexed under every step.

At the threshold it stopped. It stood in the dark of the barn with the sun in front of it, a shape against the white, and for a very long time it simply *looked*.

Mags crawled.

She went on her hands and one good knee along the cinderblock to the keeper's door, through a gap she had used ten thousand times, into the narrow service passage that ran along the east wall of the barn. She did not look back. She got the door closed behind her, silently, and she got her back against it, and she slid down it to the floor and pressed both fists against her mouth, and she shook.

*Don't. Not yet. Later.*

She got up.

* * *

The passage let out at the side yard, behind the keeper's shed and a stand of acacia, and from there she could see the whole of the African Plains laid out in the thin, cold sunshine like a map.

The visitors were still at the rail.

Not all of them. Carla, the educator, in her green uniform and her headset, had gathered most of the school group and was walking them up the path toward the Carousel Court in a long, cheerful, nervous line. "Okay, friends, we're going to take a little walk, and nobody run!" But there were still forty or fifty people at the moat, clustered against the railing in clumps, and none of them looked frightened. They looked puzzled. They looked at the barn the way people look at a thing they have been told to be interested in.

A man had his phone up. A teenager was filming with both hands. A woman in a puffy coat had lifted a toddler onto her hip to see.

"Get away from the rail," Mags said.

It came out as a croak. No one heard.

Across the yard, Imani had left the shade.

She stood in the center of the Plains, in the open, in the full sun. Her ears were spread to their widest, a gray sail on each side of her head, and her trunk was up, curled high, tasting the air. Behind her, Zola, Imani's daughter, twenty-two, big and uncertain, had turned her body to shield Pip, the four-year-old calf, whose head poked out from behind his mother's back leg and whose little trunk was waving.

And between the barn and the people, in a straight line, with her feet planted, stood the old girl.

She was making a sound. A low rumble, felt more than heard, a purring bass note that made Mags's chest ache. The sound elephants made for each other. The sound they made when they were calling the family close, and when they were calling out the hunter, and she knew from long experience which one this was.

*Get behind me.*

"Mags!" It was Gerry, running, as much as a man of seventy-three could, across the grass with his clicker clenched in his fist and his vest flapping. "Mags, they won't come off the rail! Carla's got half and the rest won't *move*, and the thing, the noise, what *was* that—"

"Gerry. The road. The gate. How many are on the rail?"

"Forty. Maybe fifty." He bent over, hands on his knees, wheezing. "I told them. I told them it was a drill."

"Tell them again. Louder. Tell them *now*."

He looked at her face. He did not ask. He straightened up and walked, without running, toward the rail, and he spoke in the clear, slow voice of a man who had once stood on a platform at night with a lantern in his hand and told a train to stop. "Folks. Folks. I need you to move away from the railing and walk, *walk*, toward the Carousel. Now, please. Right now."

A few people turned. A few people moved.

And behind them, the great sliding door of the elephant barn, twenty feet tall, two tons of steel, rolled on its rails with a wrenching shriek and slammed sideways off its track into the wall.

The sunlight reached into the barn.

It did not reach all the way. For one more heartbeat the thing in the doorway stood in the shadow of the lintel, a dark mass under the beam with a pale glint of eyes. Then it took a step. And another. And it came out of the barn into the open air, and into the light.

Mags had thought she understood its size. She had been wrong. In the dark of the feed room it had been enormous. In the sun, it was a different order of thing. It was longer than a city bus. It was taller than the barn door. Its head swung level with the roofline, and each footfall left a depression in the packed earth, a hollow the size of a dinner table. The feathers on its neck lifted in the breeze. Its eyes, in the sunlight, were gold.

The crowd at the railing had stopped.

It was strange, afterward, how silent it was. Forty people, fifty. A child's voice, very clear, said, "Mommy, what's that?" Nobody laughed. Nobody screamed. There was a long, bright, suspended moment in which an entire crowd of people looked at the thing in the Plains, and each of them, individually, privately, rearranged the world to make room for it.

Then the phone in the teenager's hands slipped, and hit the concrete, and a woman began to scream.

It broke over the rail in a wave. People ran, all of them, in every direction, a stampede of puffy coats and strollers and shrieking children, and the thing's head swung toward the sound, toward the *movement*, with a flick like a snake's.

Imani trumpeted again.

She did not run. She lowered her head and walked forward, one step, another, until she stood twenty yards from the thing's chest, and she raised her trunk to the sky and *blared*, a sound like a train whistle in a canyon, so loud that the barn windows buzzed in their frames.

The gold eyes found her.

On the kopje, on the pile of boulders at the far end of the yard, a lion rose out of the grass. He was big, scarred, heavy in the shoulder, with a mane gone ragged at the edges. He stood on the highest rock with the sun behind him. Below him, one by one, three tawny shapes slid out of the shade and came down into the open, low, spread, moving at a walk.

Mags put her hand against the acacia and stood there, in the cold, with her heart in her mouth.

She had spent thirty years in this place. She had done a great many things in it, and she had been afraid of none of them. She had never once been afraid for Imani.

"Oh, old girl," Mags whispered. "Don't."

The thing opened its jaws, and for the first time in her life, and the first time in sixty-six million years, a Tyrannosaurus stood under an open sky and *tasted* it.
