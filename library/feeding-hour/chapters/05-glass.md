*Saturday, 1:53 p.m.*

The dark in the tunnel lasted eleven seconds. Priya counted them.

It was not a thing she decided to do. Her mind simply began, the way it did on exam mornings and in the back of ambulances, to keep a ledger. *One. Two. Three.* A child began to cry somewhere on her left. A man said, in a high, jovial, frightened voice, "Well, that's the zoo for you." Arjun's hand in hers was perfectly still, and that frightened her more than the crying.

*Eight. Nine.*

The emergency lights came on at eleven: a strip of dim red along the floor, a few amber bulbs in the ceiling, enough to see shapes by and not enough to see faces. Thirty people stood or sat in a long curved tube of glass with the afternoon pouring down through the roof, and none of them knew what to do with their hands.

"Okay," said Eddie Marsh. "Okay, everybody. That's just the power. It does this. It's, ah, it's an old grid. Nothing to worry about."

He said it well. He said it with a smile that showed too many teeth. He had gone slightly gray in the red light, and he had one hand on the radio at his shoulder, and Priya noticed that the other hand had found the flush steel door in the tunnel wall and was resting against it, the way a swimmer rests a hand on the edge of a pool.

"Is it the power or is it the tiger?" said a boy of about twelve, in a Giants jacket. "Because she's looking at something."

She was. Katya had not moved from the place where she stood, ten feet from the glass, her head low and her shoulders high, and she was looking at the green door in the wall behind the boulders. Priya had never seen an animal so completely occupied by a single thing. It was as if the rest of the world had gone out with the lights.

"Tiger's fine," Eddie said. "She's just, she hears the generator."

"There's no generator noise," the boy said.

Nobody said anything to that.

Priya looked at the stream that ran past the glass. The water was still shivering. But it was not the shiver of a moment ago, the all-at-once tremble of something very heavy set down far away. It had a rhythm now. Long beat, short gap, long beat. She could feel it in the soles of her feet and in the bench under her, and, when she held still, in the hollow of her chest, like a slow second pulse that did not belong to her.

She had read about this. She tried to remember where. *Infrasound.* It was a thing animals did, elephants and whales and, she thought, tigers. A sound too low for the ear that you felt in your body before you knew it was there. A girl in her bio class had written a paper on it and had put in a sentence about how some people, in some old houses, had come to believe they were haunted, when all that was wrong was a fan in the attic, running at a certain speed.

*It isn't a fan.*

"Eddie," she said. "Is that normal? The vibration?"

He looked at her. For a moment he did not seem to know whom he was talking to. "I don't," he said. "No. It isn't."

His radio crackled.

"...any staff, any staff," said a woman's voice, thin, high, the words tumbling. "This is Ops. We have a situation. Gates are locked. All visitors back to the center, please, as fast as you can do it safely. Repeat, all staff, bring your visitors to the center. I don't know why. Just do it."

A silence settled over the tunnel.

"What's she mean, the gates are locked?" someone said.

"It means," said a woman at the back, dryly, "that we're not leaving."

It was a plain, tired, pleasant voice, and Priya turned. A woman in her thirties in a camel coat sat on the bench by the wall, a paper coffee cup in one hand, the same one that had rattled against the wood a minute and a half ago. She had a round, kind face and a pen stuck in her hair. She said, to Priya, in the voice of a woman who had spent a great many years talking to scared children, "Sorry. That wasn't helpful. I'm Denise. Is that your brother?"

"Arjun," Arjun said.

"Hi, Arjun. Hold on to your sister."

"I am," Arjun said, with dignity.

"Okay, everybody," Eddie said. "Let's, we're going to do this calmly. Ops says visitors to the center. We'll walk out the way we came in. All the way up the trail, past the spruce, and onto the main path. I'll lead. The tunnel exit's just there, past the second bench, and I'd like you to take your time. Children by the hand. No running."

He had the radio in his hand now. "Ops, this is Eddie at Taiga. I've got thirty-one visitors in the tunnel. We're coming up the trail. The, ah, the lights are out. Is there anything else I should know? Ops?"

Static.

"Bev?"

"Eddie," said Denise, gently.

He turned.

She had not moved from the bench. She was looking, past him, through the curved glass, at the yard. "Eddie. What is that door?"

Priya turned, and so did everyone else.

The green steel door in the low brick wall was not moving. It was not doing anything at all. But it was bulging. The surface of it, a flat steel plate painted park green, had bowed slowly outward from the middle, like a sheet of paper under a thumb, and as she watched, the bow deepened. The ferns around the frame shivered. A small stream of dust ran down the bricks.

"That door goes to a pump room," Eddie said, in a thin quiet voice. "It's bolted. It's been bolted since before I got here."

Katya made a sound.

Priya had never heard a tiger roar. She had heard it on television, a thing of speakers and echo and menace, a lion with a bad temper. This was not that. She *felt* it before she heard it. It hit her in the chest like a wave. It came through the glass and the floor and the bench, a vast, low, rolling shudder of sound with a rasp at the bottom of it, a note that seemed to come from everywhere at once and that left her ears ringing and her heart knocking. Beside her, Arjun clapped both hands over his chest. A woman screamed. The glass of the tunnel shook in its frames.

The door burst.

It did not fly. It folded, with a clang that cracked across the yard, and tore out of the wall with a spray of brick, and hung for a second from a single hinge. Behind it was a black rectangle and, in the black, something *moved*.

Katya did not charge. Priya would remember that. She stood in a low crouch with her belly almost to the ground, between the broken door and the dark brick bulk of the night house at the back of the yard where her cubs were shut in, and she did not take one step in either direction. She had made her decision long before the door gave. She had decided where she was going to stand, and she stood there.

Something stepped out of the dark and into the light.

* * *

It was feathered.

That was the first thing, and it seemed to Priya, in a calm corner of her mind that was still keeping its ledger, to be a stupid thing to notice. But she had been told all her life that dinosaurs were scales, and what stepped out of that door was covered, from the crown of its head to the base of its tail, in feathers: dark brown and gray, with a pale ruff along the throat, and a streak of rust where the neck met the shoulders, like a hawk's. They lay close and sleek. They moved a little in the air.

It was about twenty feet long. Most of that was tail, a long, straight, stiff rod of it held out behind like a rudder, with a plume of long feathers at the tip. It stood as high as a man's chest. It walked on two legs, with its body level and its head forward, and it put its feet down one at a time in a long, light, precise stride, and on each foot the second toe was held up and curled back off the ground, and on the end of that toe was a claw.

A sickle. A black, curved, glistening blade as long as a kitchen knife.

The head was long and narrow, with a low brow and a mouth full of small, sharp teeth. The eyes were large, and they were set to the front, and they were *bright*. They went from the broken door to the tigress to the boulders to the stream, with a swift, hungry, interested intelligence that Priya had seen before in only one kind of animal. She had seen it in crows. She had seen it in the cat on her street, the one the woman in the blue coat had saved, in the moment before it ran.

The creature lifted one forelimb, a hand with three long fingers hooked like grapnels, and flexed it in the air, slowly, as though trying out a thing it had only just learned it could do.

"It's a Utahraptor," said Arjun.

His voice was perfectly clear. It carried through the tunnel like a bell. Everyone looked at him.

"It's not a Velociraptor," he said. "Velociraptors were the size of turkeys. This is a *Utahraptor ostrommaysorum.* It's a dromaeosaur. It's from the Early Cretaceous. It's about a thousand pounds. Nobody knows if they hunted in groups, but there's a bone bed in Utah, so some people think so." He swallowed. His hand had gone cold in Priya's. "It has a killing claw on each foot. It's an ambush hunter. It, it jumps on you. And it holds on with the claws and it, it eats you while you're still."

"Arjun," Priya said. "Stop."

"That's what it does," Arjun said, very small. "That's what the book says."

On the far side of the glass, the tigress and the dinosaur looked at each other.

It was Katya who moved first. She went in a single flat, silent, ripping line across thirty feet of grass, a streak of orange and black and white, her whole body stretched like a spring, and she hit the creature at the shoulder with a sound like a car door slamming. They went down together in a roll of fur and feather. A cloud of dust and torn grass boiled up around them. Something bright and thin sang through the air and Priya realized that it was Katya's blood.

The thing was fast. She had not expected that. It had three hundred pounds of tiger on its chest, and it *twisted*, in a motion like a snake's, and got its feet up under her. Priya saw the sickle claws go in. She saw Katya scream, a high, shocked, furious scream, and rake, and bite. She saw a mass of feathers go flying. She saw the creature's tail whip over the grass, a long brown lash, and strike the glass of the tunnel with a crack that lit the whole yard in a white star.

"Back!" Eddie shouted. "Everyone *back*, away from the glass!"

The tunnel erupted. People ran in both directions, a tide of coats and shoes and screams, and in the middle of it a woman fell and a man trod on her hand. Priya got Arjun against the wall with her body in front of him and held him there.

"Look at me," she said. "Arjun. Look at me. Don't look at them."

"I'm not scared."

"I know."

"I'm *not*."

"I know you aren't," Priya said, and she heard her own voice shake.

In the yard, the dinosaur had thrown the tigress off. It rose out of the churned dirt with a dozen streaks of blood down its flank and its feathers soaked dark, and for a moment the two animals stood facing each other, six feet apart, both panting. Katya was bleeding from a long, ragged gash across her ribs. She did not look at the wound. She did not look at the people. She did not look at anything but the dinosaur, with a look that was not rage, exactly. It was the look of a mother who has already made up her mind.

And the creature, for the first time, seemed to understand that it was not going to win this the easy way.

It turned its head.

It looked, not at the tiger, but past her, at the glass. At the long curved tube of people. At the thirty warm shapes, jostling, shouting, easy.

Priya felt the look land on her.

"Oh no," Denise said, quietly, from the floor.

The creature went up the boulders in three bounds. Priya had never seen anything move so lightly. It came to the top of the highest rock, ten feet above the roof of the tunnel, and stood there with its tail out behind and its head low, and it *looked down*. The glass of the ceiling was thick, laminated, an inch and a half of it, built to take the weight of snow and falling branches. It was greenish and beautiful with the sun on it.

The creature leaped.

It landed in a crouch, with all four limbs spread, on the very center of the roof. The glass boomed like a drum. A long white crack shot out from under its feet like lightning down a tree, and the whole ceiling flexed, and a fine glittering dust sifted down onto the benches. The creature lifted one foot, curious, and brought it down again.

"Go," Eddie said. "Go. *Go.* Everyone to the exit, right now, run!"

The second leap was a short one. The sickle claw went into the glass like an ice pick into a frozen pond, and the crack became a web, and the web became a hole. It was the size of a tabletop. It opened in a slow, stately, almost graceful collapse, and a great slab of laminated glass fell in a single sheet onto the bench where Denise had been sitting.

Denise had not run. She had gotten up. She had stood up from the bench with her coffee cup still in one hand, because a boy had fallen in front of her, the boy in the Giants jacket, and she had bent to pick him up. She had one arm around his waist and she was lifting him over the fallen glass, and she said, in the plain calm voice of a woman who had talked to scared children for years, "Up you go, sweetheart, up you go."

The creature dropped through the hole.

It was the quietest thing Priya had ever seen. It landed on four feet in the middle of the aisle with a soft, heavy thud, a thousand pounds of it, in a shower of glass. Its head swung left. It saw the boy, who had been thrown clear and was scrambling away on his hands and knees. It saw the woman bending over him.

It struck.

Priya did not see all of it. She saw the foot come up. She saw the black curved claw sweep out and down, and she heard the sound it made, and she saw a paper coffee cup go spinning across the floor, still full, trailing a long brown ribbon, and strike the glass wall and burst.

She did not see the rest. Her brother's face was in her coat. She had turned his head, with one hand, and pressed it there, and she was holding it. *Don't look.* Over her shoulder she could hear the sound of something heavy dropped on the floor, and a long, bubbling, wordless noise, and then a second noise, which was worse, which was the sound of silence.

"Priya." Eddie had her by the arm. His face was a white blur. "*Priya.* This way."

He had the door open.

It was the flush steel door in the tunnel wall. It stood wide on a narrow gray corridor lit by a single bulb. She saw a mop bucket, a rack of coats, a poster for a charity run. The boy in the Giants jacket was being pushed through by his father. Behind them, the tunnel was a roaring tangle of coats and screams, and past it, forty feet away in the dim red light, with its head down and its feathers dripping, a shape was turning from the floor toward the people, one by one, as if counting.

"Leave the stroller," Eddie said. "Leave the bags. Leave it. Go."

Priya pulled Arjun into the corridor and did not look back.

She was a person who thought it through. She had always been. She had thought it through when the cat was hit and when her father called and when the tuition numbers in the binder had begun to frighten her. She had a plan for every occasion. She had never in her life been in a place where there was nothing to plan.

So she did the only thing that she could. She counted.

*One. Two.* The door swung. Eddie's hand on the frame. *Seven. Eight.* A woman with a baby. A man who was crying. The thing in the tunnel lifted its head and looked down the corridor, directly at her, with a bright, sidelong, curious eye.

*Nine.*

Eddie slammed the door.

And the whole steel plate rang like a struck bell.
