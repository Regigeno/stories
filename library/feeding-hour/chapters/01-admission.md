*Saturday, 12:41 p.m.*

The woman at the ticket window looked at Priya's crumpled bills, then at Arjun, who was standing on his toes to see over the counter, and said, "Is somebody having a birthday?"

"Me," Arjun said. "I'm nine. As of today. Officially."

"Then you get a sticker." She slid it through the gap in the glass, a green one with a cartoon giraffe on it, and Arjun held it between two fingers like a medal he was not sure he should accept. He was a boy who had asked for a Tyrannosaurus skeleton kit for his birthday and received instead a Saturday at a zoo with no dinosaurs in it. Priya had braced herself for that disappointment on the bus. So far he was being gracious about it, which worried her more.

"Two admissions, one child under twelve," the woman said. "Thirty-one dollars."

Priya paid. That left her with eleven dollars and some change from three weeks of babysitting the Okafor twins, who had once flushed a sock down the toilet to see what would happen. The plan for the eleven dollars was a pretzel to share and, if there was enough left, the little plastic dinosaur from the gift shop that Arjun would pretend not to want. She had thought it through. She always thought it through. Their mother called it her "spreadsheet brain" and said it like a compliment, usually on the nights when she was too tired to say anything else.

"Stay where I can see you," Priya said, and they went through the turnstile.

The main gate opened onto a wide plaza paved in brick with a carousel at the center of it, painted animals going up and down to a tune that had been slightly out of tune for decades. Kids were screaming happily on the carousel. Parents were screaming less happily at strollers. It was the last Saturday in October, bright and cold, with the kind of blue sky that made everything look cut out of paper, and the zoo was packed. A banner above the food court said FALL FESTIVAL, and somebody in a lion costume was posing for photographs while sweating visibly through the mane.

"There's the map," Arjun said, and ran to it.

He had memorized the website last night. Priya knew because he had recited it to her at one in the morning from the top bunk, in the voice he used for things that mattered: *African Plains, Okavango Lagoon, Taiga Trail, Arctic Coast, Giraffe Overlook.* He had left out the Reptile House, which was on the map, in the middle, with a small gray rectangle over it.

"Closed for renovation," Priya read.

"It's been closed forever." Arjun said it with the authority of someone who had read forty reviews. "A kid on the forum said two years. They only open the front for school groups, and then it's just a hallway with a banner."

"What's it a banner of?"

"Frogs, probably. That's what's in there. Frogs and snakes." He shrugged. "Snakes are okay. Lizards are okay. They're not dinosaurs, though. People think they are, but dinosaurs have a different hip."

"I know," Priya said. "You told me."

"I told you twice."

"I remember both."

He grinned at her, quick and gap-toothed, and for a moment he was the Arjun she knew and not the strange small diplomat who had been quietly managing everyone's feelings all week. Their father was supposed to be here. He had traded two shifts at the hospital to be here. And then someone on the respiratory floor had called out sick and he had said, over the phone at eleven that morning, in his careful English that got more careful whenever he was sorry, that he would make it up, that he would make it up to both of them. Arjun had said it was fine. He had said it so quickly that Priya had known right then how much it wasn't.

So: the zoo. She had a map, a pretzel budget, and a younger brother. That was enough to build a day on.

They went clockwise because Priya liked to do things in a loop. The path curved past the flamingos, where Arjun said, "Those are also dinosaurs," and a man with a stroller gave him a look, and then past the red pandas, who were asleep in a heap in the crook of a tree and did not care about anything. Priya stood at the glass longer than she meant to. She had wanted to be a veterinarian since she was eight, since the afternoon a cat had been hit on their street and a woman in a blue coat had come out of nowhere and knelt on the asphalt with her hands inside the cat's fur, steady, murmuring. She had taken the AP Biology exam in May. She had a three-ring binder at home labeled VET SCHOOL, with tabs. She had not yet told anyone how much the tuition numbers in it frightened her.

"Priya." Arjun was tugging her sleeve. "Priya. Look at the fence."

"What about it?"

"It's new."

She looked. They were passing the edge of the Reptile House, a squat brick building from the nineteen-thirties with arched windows and a green copper roof, its front doors chained. Around the back, where a service road ran between the building and a hill of dead grass, a chain-link fence stood eight feet tall with a roll of razor wire on top. The wire was bright. It had not been rained on yet.

"Maybe they're renovating," she said.

"With razor wire?"

"To keep people out of the construction."

"Then where's the construction?"

It was a fair question. There was no scaffolding, no dumpsters, no portable toilets, none of the clutter that came with a building being torn up. There was, however, a white box truck parked at the end of the service road with its back door raised, and two men in gray coveralls unloading a pallet of something wrapped in plastic. Cold, she thought. The plastic was fogged from the inside, and the men were handling it like it was breakable and expensive. A third man stood by the truck with his arms folded and looked directly at Priya.

He did not look away when she looked back. He had the flat, patient look of a man whose job was to be looked at and not to react. After a few seconds he turned his head, and spoke into something on his collar, and went around the truck out of sight.

"Okay, that was weird," Arjun whispered, delighted.

"It was a delivery," Priya said. "Come on."

But she took his hand, which she had not done since he was six, and he let her.

They passed the Reptile House and the path dropped into a stretch of the park the signs called AFRICAN PLAINS, where a long, curving moat of dry gray stone held back a yard as big as a football field, ocher grass, scattered acacia trees, a hill of piled boulders on the far side where a lion lay stretched on his flank like a rug somebody had dropped. Beyond him, a brick barn with a huge sliding door. A crowd lined the railing. A woman in a green uniform was speaking into a headset microphone to a ring of kids.

"...and she's forty-one years old," the woman said, "which makes her one of the oldest elephants in North America in a zoo. Imani, can you say hello?"

On the far side of the moat, in the shade of the barn, an enormous gray animal turned her head and looked at the kids as if she had known them all their lives. Priya's breath went out of her. She had seen elephants on television. It had not prepared her for how *still* one could be.

"She's huge," Arjun breathed.

"That's Mags's girl," said a man next to them, an older guy in a Calder Bay Zoo volunteer vest. "Mags has been with her since the first day. Thirty years. They say if Mags is crying, Imani knows before Mags does."

"Is that true?"

"Probably not," the volunteer said, and winked. "But it should be."

Priya watched the elephant. Imani had lifted her trunk, the way a person might lift a hand to test the wind. The tip of it moved slowly, side to side. Then she lowered it and shifted her weight from one forefoot to the other, and then back, and for a second Priya had the odd impression that the elephant was listening to something under the ground.

"Is she okay?" Priya asked.

"She does that sometimes," the volunteer said. "The new plumbing, the keepers think. Old pipes. She feels everything."

"What new plumbing?"

"Beats me. They've had trucks coming and going for a year." He lowered his voice, the way people do when they are about to tell you something they have already told twenty strangers. "Rumor is it's a research wing. Conservation. Rare-species breeding. Real hush-hush."

"What species?"

"If they told me, hon, it wouldn't be hush-hush."

Priya laughed politely. The ground, under her sneakers, was humming.

It was faint. It was nothing, a bus on a distant road, a train. But she had been standing on this one piece of pavement for five minutes and the hum had not moved. It had the dull steadiness of a refrigerator in another room.

"Priya," Arjun said. "The tiger thing is at two. We have to go now or we'll be late."

"We have an hour."

"It's a *forty-minute* walk."

"It is not forty minutes."

"It's *twenty-five*," Arjun said, which was probably right. He had the map in his head the way some kids carried a game. She looked once more at Imani, who was now very still, with one ear flared forward, and then let her brother tow her away.

They stopped for the pretzel. It cost five dollars, which was robbery, and Arjun ate three-quarters of it before Priya got her share. They walked past the Okavango Lagoon, where a long pier ran out over green water and a boat the shape of a pontoon with a striped canopy was loading passengers. A sign said HIPPO HARBOR CRUISES, $6. Arjun looked at the sign. Priya looked at the six dollars in her pocket that she had already allocated to the little plastic dinosaur.

"Maybe after," she said.

"Maybe after," he agreed, with the dignity of a person being brave, and they kept walking.

The man piloting the boat was lean, thirtyish, in a faded jacket, with a face that looked like it had been left outside a long time. He was counting passengers aboard, one hand moving, lips moving. He glanced up as they passed. Priya, for no reason she could name, nodded to him. He nodded back.

Taiga Trail was cooler than the rest of the park. The planners had put in a stand of real spruce, and the path wound into it the way a trail in a forest would, narrow and shaded, with a smell of cold needles. The sound of the carousel faded. A hand-painted wooden sign read TAIGA TRAIL: HOME OF THE AMUR TIGER, and under it, in smaller letters, *Feeding Talk: Saturdays, 2 p.m.* Someone had added a smiley face, in marker, to the *2*.

"We're early," Priya said, relieved. It was 1:31.

"Early is on time," Arjun said, which was what their mother always said. He took a dinosaur-shaped gummy out of his pocket and ate it, and then looked, with sudden guilt, at his sister. "I had two of those. Sorry. They were for later."

"Happy birthday," Priya said.

The trail ran straight into the enclosure through a long tunnel of glass. It was a real tunnel, a curved tube set into the hillside, with a rubber floor and benches along one wall, and the tiger yard wrapped around it on every side: a stream, boulders, a pair of fallen trees, long yellow grass. The ceiling was glass. The light came through it green and gold. There were maybe thirty people in there already, kids pressed to the curved glass, a few parents with phones held up in front of their faces like shields.

And there was the tiger.

She was lying along a flat rock about four feet from the glass, her chin on her paws, and she was bigger than any picture had suggested. Not just long. *Dense*. Her coat was a pale, thick orange that went nearly white along her belly, and the black stripes lay across her like something that had been drawn with a fine brush and then regretted. She was looking at nothing. The tip of her tail twitched once every few seconds.

"That's Katya," said a voice behind them. "She's fourteen. Please don't tap on the glass, she hates it. She won't do anything about it, but she'll remember."

A young man in a green keeper's jacket had come in through a door set flush into the tunnel wall. He was tall, slightly stooped, with a bag of something that smelled strongly of meat in one hand and a clipboard in the other, and he had the harried, apologetic look of somebody who had been told to smile for the public and was working on it.

"I'm Eddie," he said. "I'll be talking about tigers at two, but I'm early, so if anybody wants to ask me stuff now, I'm allowed to answer unofficially."

Arjun's hand shot up so fast Priya felt the breeze.

"Yes. Hi. Birthday sticker, nice. What's your question?"

"Are tigers dinosaurs?"

Eddie blinked. "...No?"

"I didn't think so. I just wanted to check," Arjun said, serenely. "They're mammals. They have fur. Birds are the dinosaurs."

"That's right," Eddie said, and then, because he was a decent person, he added, "Good one."

Priya felt her shoulders loosen for the first time that day. The tunnel was warm. The tiger lay in her pool of green light. A little girl somewhere behind them was singing the carousel tune, off-key in exactly the same way. In an hour they would go find the little plastic dinosaur. In two they'd catch the bus. Tonight Dad would come home and she would tell him it had been good and he would believe her, partly.

"You said two cubs, right?" a woman asked Eddie. "On the website, it said there were two."

"Yearlings, yes. Misha and Dasha." Eddie checked his watch. "They're in the back, in the night house, because they get a bit riotous and I want you to see Mom at her best first. Katya, say hi."

The tigress did not say hi. She did not lift her head. But her ears, which had been turned loosely outward, rotated slowly forward, like a pair of small, patient radar dishes, toward the back of the yard.

Eddie frowned. "That's odd."

"What is?" Arjun said.

"She's usually, ah." He shrugged, but his smile had thinned. "She usually ignores the pipes. The new construction. There's a lot of noise from under the hill sometimes. She hates it, but she's gotten used to it. This is different."

"Different how?" Priya asked.

Eddie did not answer. He was looking at the surface of the stream that ran past the glass. Priya followed his gaze.

The water was trembling. Not rippling, not the way it did in wind. It was shivering all at once, in tight little concentric rings, as though something very heavy had been set down a long way off and the whole yard were feeling it through its feet.

On the bench next to her, a woman's paper coffee cup rattled against the wood.

"Is that a truck?" somebody said.

"It's a truck," someone else said, uncertainly.

The tigress had stood up.

She stood all at once, without stretching, which Priya had a feeling was not something tigers did, and she was not looking at the glass anymore. She was looking at a low brick wall behind the boulders, where a steel door painted the same green as the rest of the park stood half hidden in the ferns. Her whole body had changed. Her head hung low, and her shoulders rose, and the tip of her tail, which had been twitching, was now perfectly still.

"Okay," Eddie said softly, to nobody. "Okay, Katya. What is it?"

From somewhere deep under the hill, beyond the glass and the grass and the pretzel and the paper-cut sky, something enormous gave a long, low groan, as if the earth itself had finally been asked a question it did not want to answer.

Then the lights in the tunnel went out.

Arjun's hand found hers in the dark. It was small and warm and sticky with sugar. Priya looked at the clock on her phone, out of habit, because she was a person who thought it through, and she would remember afterward that she had seen the numbers clearly, white on black, and that the little signal bars beside them had already gone.

It was 1:52.
