*1987 to the Unconfirmed Years, beneath the plaster stars*

Before anyone measured the room in borders, there was only the carpet, the oak, and the light through the blinds.

The carpet lay at the center of the world, a broad oval of braided wool dyed in fading rings of slate blue and pine green. Around its fringes ran wide planks of varnished oak, cold after sundown and bright as amber whenever morning sun cut sideways through the white venetian blinds. Along the north wall stood the cast-iron radiator, ticking and sighing through the winter months like an old lung. To the west rose the walnut cliff of the dresser, its brass drawer-pulls hanging high overhead like dull moons, and beside it sat a white-painted wooden toy chest with chipped corners and heavy brass hinges. To the east stood a low bookcase packed with picture books, cloth-bound storybooks, and a row of stout encyclopedias; next to it was the white closet door, whose threshold let in thin drafts from an unreachable hall, and a small wooden school desk strewn with pencil shavings and stubs of wax crayon. Along the southern rim loomed the twin bed, its wooden posts climbing into the gloom and its plaid dust-ruffle hanging to within a hair's breadth of the floorboards, sealing off the dark beneath.

Above it all stretched the white plaster sky, scattered with pale green glow-in-the-dark plastic stars that drank light all afternoon and breathed it back weakly into the night.

It was a small cosmos, and for a long time it was quiet. Then came the year 1987, and with it, the first flags.

## I. The First Lands (1987–1992)

They were not empires yet—only two peoples, the Shell Tribe and the Car Tribe, the first countries the world had ever known.

The Shells claimed the inner loops of the braided rug, where the wool lay thick enough to muffle their ridged green and red carapaces as they shifted camp. They were compact, armored, slow to trust, built to endure impact and hold ground. The Cars settled along the outer oak border where the wood ran smooth beneath their axles, die-cast metal and molded plastic rolling in tight formations from the doorway to the foot of the bookcase. Both peoples coveted the borderland where wool met varnish: the Shells for the flat, clear approaches it offered; the Cars for the cover of the braided fringe when the afternoon glare turned the open planks blinding.

At first there were only boundary markers—a bent paperclip wedged into a floor seam, a chalk stroke scraped beside the rug's hem. Then a Shell sentry was found cracked along his rim near the oak, his green ridge split clean through. Two mornings later a Car scout rolled back into camp with a bent front axle and chipped enamel along his hood. Envoys met over the bent paperclip as the yellow bars of sunlight crept eastward across the wool.

"Your tires crossed the blue ring before dawn," the Shell speaker said, his voice dry and flat beneath his rim.

"Your people threw the first block on the oak," the Car envoy answered, wheels locked tight on the floorboards. "You know where the seam lies."

Neither stepped back. Tension did not climb by degrees; it snapped. Within weeks both tribes were throwing up barricades of loose dominoes, wooden ruler-ends, and fallen pencils, striking at night beneath the dim green glow of the ceiling stars. Neither side could break the other, and neither could live in the rubble of the central carpet. The wool tore in gray tufts under grinding treads; oil and painted dust stained the oak. By the time the radiator went cold that spring, the First Lands were unlivable. Exhausted and hollowed out, both the Shell Tribe and the Car Tribe packed their dead and abandoned the ground they had once called home, scattering into the shadowed margins along the baseboards.

Long afterward, historians would ask which border post fell first, whose envoy lied, whose tire or carapace crossed the hem of the rug in the dark. Nobody knew. Even the veterans who survived into the next decade could only point at the bare center of the carpet and swear that the other side had begun it.

* * *

In 1988, while the Shells and Cars were still nursing their exile along the walls, ruin came to the east side of the room.

The Mushroom Kingdom had stood quietly at the foot of the bookcase, built from painted pine blocks, red-and-white cardboard arches, and folded notebook-paper tapestries pinned against the lower shelf. It was small, orderly, and proud of its peace. At its heart ruled Princess Peach, dressed in molded pink enamel and a thin gold crown, holding court beneath an arched paper canopy where afternoon light fell through the blinds in neat amber bars.

No invading army brought that kingdom down. It died from the inside, in silence.

Mario had walked those block corridors for years—short, heavy-shouldered, dressed in red cap and dark blue overalls, his painted mustache stern above a mouth that rarely showed what sat behind it. Whatever ambition or private grievance had been fermenting beneath that steady stare, he kept it sealed until a still evening when the radiator clicked off and the palace guards stood sparse along the lower steps. He climbed alone to the upper block chamber, where Peach sat looking out toward the dim expanse of the braided rug.

There were no witnesses to what was said, if anything was said at all. No trial, no proclamation, no coup announced from the balcony. Mario killed Princess Peach in secret, leaving the pale pink hem of her gown still in the shadow of the lower shelf.

Then he did not try to rule what he had taken. He brought fire.

Whether it was a stray kitchen match dragged out from behind the radiator or a dry strip of paper touched to the scalding metal shade of the bedside lamp, the flame caught the cardboard arches instantly. Paper banners curled black and lifted in small glowing flakes toward the plaster stars. The varnished pine blocks blistered and split; smoke pooled along the lower spines of the picture books, acrid with melted paint and scorched glue. By the time the fire burned itself out against the cold oak planks, the palace was nothing but warped charcoal and gray ash, leaving a dark half-moon stain on the floorboard that never came clean. Stripped of its sovereign and its seat of government in a single night, the Mushroom Kingdom collapsed completely. Its citizens dispersed into the shadows beneath the school desk and behind the toy chest, and for three years no one spoke Mario's name above a whisper.

* * *

Out of that ash, in 1989, rose the first true empire the bedroom had ever seen.

It took root at the foot of the walnut dresser, where the light fell steady and the wooden cliff behind offered an unassailable back wall. Its name—the Funky Empire—sounded strange even to those who swore allegiance to it, a bright, stubborn banner raised in a room that had already learned the smell of smoke. Stranger still was the figure who founded it.

He called himself Captain America, and he was a knockoff.

Anyone who stood close enough in good daylight could see the truth of his making. The white five-pointed star on his chest was stamped a millimeter off-center. A thin vertical flash-line from a cheap factory mold ran down both of his blue flanks. His legs were hollow, lighter than they looked, his left elbow stiff in its socket, and the round shield strapped to his forearm was a thin disc of slightly dull red and faded blue that rang hollow when struck against wood. He had never come from a gilded collector's box or an honored shelf; he had been stamped by the thousands in some nameless press and tossed into a bin.

Perhaps that was why he insisted so fiercely on law.

Captain America built the Funkies' Palace from heavy wooden blocks and stacked hardcover encyclopedias, ordering the walls squared to the dresser's edge, setting watches by the slant of the blinds, and recording rations and borders on torn slips of lined school paper. He spoke quietly, listened longer than he spoke, and never pretended to be made of finer plastic than his soldiers. Because he knew how flimsy a seam could be, he prized endurance above swagger. Within months, displaced settlements along the west wall swore fealty to his banner. In 1989, by formal proclamation read from the steps of the encyclopedia citadel, the Funky Empire was declared the first empire introduced to the world.

* * *

A year later, in 1990, the northern floor stirred.

Along the cool oak strip beneath the radiator, the scattered remnants of the Shell Tribe began to gather again. They were no longer the frantic, disorganized clans that had fled the braided rug three years earlier. A new leader had emerged among them: Lakitu, perched in a small white plastic cloud mounted on a clear curved stem that lifted him half a head above every soldier in his ranks. Behind his thick painted goggles sat a cool, measuring mind. From his elevated perch he could see clean across the braided carpet to the dresser, the bookcase, and the bedroom door.

Under Lakitu, the Shells reorganized into the Shell Empire, fortifying the radiator pipes and disciplining their armored phalanxes. Almost immediately, before suspicion could harden across the wool, Captain America marched an unarmed delegation north from the dresser. Lakitu drifted forward on his cloud to meet him on the cool oak. Both leaders understood the same hard truth: the room was full of ghosts and opportunists, and an empire that stood alone would eventually burn like the Mushroom Palace. Within days the Funky Empire and the Shell Empire signed a formal alliance—a pact of mutual defense that bound the west wall to the north.

They did not have long to wait before it was tested.

* * *

In 1991, Mario returned.

He emerged from the dim eastern margin near the charred stain of the old Mushroom Palace, no longer a solitary assassin but the ruler of a disciplined, ruthless state—the Mario Empire, forged from opportunists, hardened survivors, and soldiers who admired the sheer coldness of what he had done in 1988. Soot still lingered faintly in the creases of his blue overalls.

He approached the Funkies' Palace under a white strip of folded tissue, requesting an audience with Captain America. In the dim hall of stacked encyclopedias, Mario spoke smoothly of stability, shared trade across the braided rug, and an alliance that would unite the west and east walls.

"The east wall has seen enough fire, Captain," Mario said, standing square on the oak step, his painted eyes unreadable. "Let us divide the floor in peace before someone worse rises between us."

Captain America listened with his chipped shield resting across his knee, his face still, and agreed to review the terms overnight.

Mario did not wait for the ink to dry.

At dawn the very next day, as the first thin bars of yellow sun slipped through the blinds, the Mario Empire threw itself against the walls of the Funkies' Palace. There had never been any intention of alliance; the parley had been nothing more than a reconnaissance of the palace gates and sentry rotations. Wooden blocks toppled in the morning stillness. Mario's shock troops poured into the lower courtyard, driving back the stunned Funky guards before the alarm bells—brass buttons struck with bent nails—could even finish ringing.

What followed became known as the Mario War.

The Funkies held the upper steps of the encyclopedia fortress through sheer discipline, Captain America himself bracing the main arch with his thin, hollow shield until his stiff elbow nearly split at the pin. Then, across the braided rug from the north, came the steady scrape of green and red carapaces over wool. Lakitu had kept his oath. Floating above his columns like a pale sentry, he drove the Shell phalanxes straight into Mario's exposed flank.

Caught between the Funkies' counterattack and the armored weight of the Shells, the Mario Empire broke. For weeks the fighting dragged across the central carpet and back toward the eastern planks, bitter and unforgiving, until the last Mario barricade collapsed beside the scorched floorboard where Peach's palace had once stood.

Defeated, Mario and the remnants of his regime vanished into hiding. They crawled into the narrow, cobwebbed slot behind the bookcase—a sunless trench of dust-bunnies, dropped bookmarks, and cold plaster—hoping the victorious allies would tire of searching and return to their own borders.

The allies did not tire. Neither Captain America nor Lakitu had forgotten the ashes of 1988, nor the morning ambush that followed an offer of peace. Funky and Shell patrols swept the baseboards systematically, tapping hollow timber, lowering weighted string-lines behind furniture, following faint boot-scuffs in the dust behind the shelves. One gray afternoon they found the hideout.

Mario and his loyalists were dragged out into the pale strip of oak beside the bookcase. There was no second mercy, no exile to the baseboards. Under the silent watch of Funky riflemen and Lakitu's floating cloud, the leaders of the Mario Empire were lined against the white skirting board and executed. When the line went still, the east wall was quiet at last.

* * *

Peace lasted less than a year. In 1992, the sound of rubber on hardwood rolled back from the bedroom door.

The Car Tribe had endured five years of exile along the drafty southern and western edges of the room, nursing their bent axles and their old pride. Now they returned as the Car Empire, united under a single commanding chassis: an F1 racer known simply as Big Car. He was wider and heavier than any vehicle in the room, his low nosecone scarred from old collisions, his racing decals faded at the edges, his broad black tires gripping the oak with effortless weight. Where other leaders shouted, Big Car merely idled at the head of his column, his presence alone enough to straighten a wavering line.

The moment the Car Empire raised its banners along the doorway straightaway, the old poison of 1987 rose to the surface. Patrols from the Shell Empire and the Car Empire met along the fringe of the braided rug, headlights glaring at ridged shells, old slurs and older grievances flaring into skirmishes along the wool. Neither Lakitu nor Big Car could keep their border regiments wholly in check; the memory of the First Lands still burned too fiercely in both peoples.

Hoping to keep a general war from tearing his hard-won order apart, Captain America intervened. Later that same year, the Funky Empire opened a formal commission of inquiry on an overturned shoebox lid placed squarely between the dresser and the radiator. Funky clerks laid out slips of lined paper. Delegations from the Shell Empire and the Car Empire took opposite edges of the cardboard dais to testify on what had actually started the war of 1987.

The hearings dragged for days and resolved nothing.

"We held the blue yarn by right of settlement," a scarred Shell elder testified beneath Lakitu's watchful gaze. "Your outriders crushed our outer marker before the blinds were even drawn."

"Your people ambushed a supply convoy on bare oak," Big Car replied, his broad tires planted squarely on the cardboard edge. "I still carry the dent from that winter. Ask your own dead where they fell—they fell on wood, not wool."

Every witness contradicted the last; every scrap of physical evidence had been swept away by five years of dust and footfalls. At last Captain America closed his ledger, tied the pages shut with a length of gray thread, and declared the inquiry finished. Both empires left the shoebox table with their resentments untouched. The case was closed, and the origin of the oldest war in the room remained an unsolved mystery.

## II. The Dark Beneath the Bed and the Bonfire (1993–1994)

In 1993, the world learned that there were older and crueler things in the bedroom than politics.

For six years, every civilization had respected the plaid dust-ruffle hanging along the southern border of the rug. It swung faintly whenever a draft slipped under the bedroom door, revealing nothing beyond its hem except a strip of cold dark. Then a mapping party—bold scouts looking for unclaimed timber and loose metal along the southern oak—lifted the fabric edge and stepped beneath the bedframe.

They did not come back.

A search party went in after them with a pocket mirror angled to throw a pale beam from the window into the gloom, paying out a length of cotton string behind them as a guide. For an hour the watchers on the rug saw the beam of reflected light wobble across distant wooden slats and drifts of gray lint. Then the mirror tilted wildly, flashed once across something low and deep crimson moving swiftly behind a coiled jump-rope, and went dark. The guiding string snapped taut, twitched twice, and went slack. When the watchers hauled it back out into the daylight, the end was severed cleanly just inside the shadow line.

The place earned its name swiftly and solemnly: the Dungeon of Doom.

Peering low from the edge of the braided rug during the brightest hour of afternoon, observers slowly pieced together the nature of that lightless underworld. The floorboards under the bed were dusty as a tomb, cross-hatched with the pale shadows of bed-slats far overhead. Everything lost to the room over the years had drifted there and grown lethal: tangled coils of old jump-rope waiting like snares, loose bedsprings twisted into jagged metal ribs, glass marbles resting along slight inclines where a single careless step sent them rolling like avalanche stones, leaning stacks of forgotten hardcover books poised to crush anything that brushed their spines, and snapping plastic contraptions whose triggers waited unseen beneath drifts of lint.

Yet the traps were only half the terror. Careful watchers, lying flat at the edge of the carpet, eventually confirmed that the Dungeon held only one living entity.

It was a T-Rex—small, barely taller than a Funky soldier's waist, and painted a deep, arterial red. Nothing about its size made it merciful. Its narrow molded jaws were fixed permanently open in a silent snarl, its tiny claws hooked, its balance low and predatory. It moved through the maze of springs and snares with absolute mastery, never tripping a wire, never stirring a marble it did not mean to loose. When an intruder crossed beneath the plaid hem, the traps slowed them, maimed them, or cut off their retreat, and the small red T-Rex finished the work in the dark.

After a third expedition vanished without a cry, Captain America, Lakitu, and Big Car independently posted warning markers along the southern hem of the rug. No nation claimed the ground under the bed. The Dungeon of Doom remained open, silent, and undefeated—a reminder that every empire on the sunlit carpet existed only on sufferance.

* * *

Then came 1994, and the entire floor caught fire.

It began with four sudden ascensions across the open stretches of the room that had stood empty since the old wars.

In the shadowed lee of the white toy chest rose the Bandit Empire, ruled by a slack-jawed, olive-green Zombie whose painted ribs showed through a torn molded shirt and whose left knee dragged stiffly over the planks. On a bright patch of morning light near the window rose the Angel Empire, led by a slender white Unicorn with a gilded plastic horn and calm, painted dark eyes. Beside the white closet door, enclosed within neat interlocking rails of white plastic fencing, stood the Farm Empire—an agrarian commonwealth with no single king, governed collectively by its own cows, horses, sheep, and pigs around a small red barn. And along the floorboards beside the wooden school desk rose the Japanese Empire, ordered with rigid precision by a miniature Bus Driver in a navy uniform and dark peaked cap, his molded hands still posed as though gripping a wheel that was no longer there.

Almost the instant all four nations established their borders, they came together at the heart of the braided rug and started a bonfire.

They stacked dry splinters, shredded cardboard, stubs of wax crayon, and curled scraps of notebook paper into a mound in the exact center of the faded blue wool, and set it ablaze. Orange light danced wildly up the walnut cliffs of the dresser and across the white ceiling, drowning out the faint green plaster stars. Smoke drifted lazily through the slanted sunbeams. It was meant as a spectacle of a new order, four upstart powers announcing to the older empires along the walls that the center of the world belonged to them now.

Before the bonfire had burned down to gray curls, blood ruined it.

In the confusion of smoke and shifting shadows around the blaze, an assassin reached the Unicorn of the Angels. The gilded horn snapped clean against the oak; the white body went down silently in the glare of the embers. Without their sovereign, the Angel Empire did not merely falter—it collapsed on the spot. Its grief-stricken ranks scattered into the dark corners of the room before dawn, their bright enclave near the window empty forever.

Suspicion roared through the smoke like a draft. Before anyone could sort guilt from rumor, the Bus Driver of the Japanese Empire stood beside the desk, raised one stiff navy arm, and issued a formal order of attack against the Funky Empire.

The World War had begun.

Within hours, every banner in the bedroom was marching—every banner save one. Beside the closet door, the animals of the Farm Empire quietly latched their white plastic gates, gathered their herds inside the rails, and refused to move a hoof onto the burning rug. They had stood beside the fire, perhaps hoping for fellowship; now they stayed behind their fences and watched the rest of the world tear itself apart.

The Japanese columns struck hard against the Funkies' Palace from the east, disciplined and relentless. From the toy chest, the Zombie's Bandit legions poured across the carpet, looting, burning barricades, and hacking through every patrol they met. Bound by treaty, Lakitu committed the Shell Empire from the radiator wall; drawn by territorial fear and the threat along the doorway straightaway, Big Car rolled the Car Empire into the melee as well. The old feud between Cars and Shells did not vanish, yet both found themselves ground together in the same roaring furnace across the braided wool.

The Funky Empire began to bleed out.

Its block ramparts cracked under endless assaults. Its soldiers—hollow-limbed, chipped, outnumbered on two fronts—held the encyclopedia steps by inches, watching their supply lines to the Shell and Car borders snap one by one. Captain America stood on the upper terrace of the palace, his off-center chest star blackened with ash from the central bonfire, and saw clearly that courage would not save them. If the war continued as a contest of plastic and numbers, the Funky Empire would fall before the radiator turned cold.

So the Funkies turned to something no nation in the room had ever touched.

In the sealed dark behind the dresser's lowest drawer, Funky scholars and tinkerers tore apart the dead remnants of the wider world that had fallen behind the furniture over the years: leaking dry-cell batteries, fine copper coils unwound from a broken transistor radio, cracked magnifying lenses, silver foil, and scraped phosphor dust from fallen ceiling stars. They measured, tested, failed, burned their fingers, and tested again. Out of desperation and cold arithmetic, the Funkies discovered science.

And from science, they built two nuclear weapons.

They were small, dense cylinders bound in copper wire and foil, humming with a quiet, unnatural weight that made the floorboards vibrate faintly beneath them. Captain America stood over them for a long time in the dim light behind the drawer. His painted face could not change expression, yet the engineers beside him remembered how long his stiff hand rested on the rim of his cheap shield before he spoke.

"Do it," he said quietly. "End it before there is nothing left to rule."

The first device fell upon the Bandit Empire beside the toy chest. The second fell upon the Japanese Empire beside the desk.

There was no thunder like a radiator bang, no slow curling smoke like the Mushroom Palace fire. There was only a white, blinding snap that bleached every shadow in the bedroom for a heartbeat, followed by a dry concussive rush that flattened the rug fibers in two clean, pale circles. When the dust settled through the sunbeams, the Bandit Empire was gone—the Zombie and his legions erased into fine gray ash. Beside the desk, the Bus Driver's neat formations and paper banners lay charred flat against the oak, motionless forever.

Silence crashed down across the bedroom. The Shells halted on the wool. The Cars braked along the floorboards. The World War ended that very afternoon.

A few weeks later, out of the scorched rubble near the toy chest, surviving Bandits tried once more to rise. Desperate and feral, they rallied behind a towering T-Rex warlord—larger and darker than the small red phantom under the bed, a brute of jagged teeth and scarred flank who promised to tear the dresser fortress apart. The Funkies did not muster an army to meet him. They brought out a third weapon from behind the drawer, aimed it across the carpet, and detonated it without hesitation. The second Bandit rising lasted less than a morning.

* * *

Victory left the survivors hollow.

The Funky Empire had won the World War, and the Car Empire had fought beside it through the worst of the carnage, yet both realms stood on the brink of ruin. Their stockpiles of marbles, buttons, and copper pennies—the whole currency of the room—had been exhausted by months of total war and the ruinous cost of the Funky science program. Palace walls stood half-collapsed; axle grease and repair glue were gone; whole regiments existed only as names on torn notebook lists.

In the quiet gray months after the third bomb, Captain America met Big Car on the same overturned shoebox lid where the Funkies had once failed to solve the mystery of 1987. There were no clerks shouting accusations now, only two battered sovereigns surveying a scarred rug.

Neither state could carry its debts alone. Together, they signed the Act of Union, folding the Funky Empire and the Car Empire into a single colossus: the Funcar Empire.

It was a moment of rare, somber grace. Big Car had led his people out of exile, built an empire from rusted pride, and commanded the fastest battalions in the room. Yet when the union was sealed, the broad-wheeled F1 racer did not demand half the throne.

"You built the first law here," Big Car said quietly, idling beside the faded knockoff figure whose paint was already thinning along the seams. "Keep the crown. I will hold your flank."

He stepped back voluntarily to serve as Captain America's second-in-command. For a brief, hard-won season, the union held. The Shells along the radiator wall remained their steady allies. The debts were shouldered together.

And then, before the year 1994 was out, the Farm Empire vanished.

There was no flash of white light, no smoke, no sound of marching feet or snapping plastic. One evening the cows, sheep, pigs, and horses stood calmly inside their white interlocking fences beside the closet door, just as they had stood throughout the bonfire and the World War, stubbornly neutral, untouched by either fire or science. The next morning, when Funcar patrols marched past the northeast corner, there was nothing there.

Not a single broken rail. Not a scrap of red barn roof. Not a fallen hoofprint in the dust. Only a neat rectangular indentation pressed into the nap of the braided rug, slowly springing back upright as the morning sunlight moved across it.

Funcar scouts searched behind the closet door, beneath the bookcase, even up along the low shelves. Big Car drove the perimeter himself, tires whispering over bare oak. Nobody ever found a clue, a ransom note, or a body. For no reason anyone in the room could fathom, an entire nation had vanished into thin air.

Long after the craters of the World War faded into the carpet, soldiers still refused to walk across that empty square beside the closet door.

## III. The Three Suns (1994–1999)

Empires built by founders rarely survive the passing of the hands that shaped them.

Captain America had been made of cheap plastic from the first, and the years had not been kind to him. Five years of sun pouring through the bedroom blinds had bleached his blue torso to chalk; hairline cracks feathered around his hollow waist; his stiff elbow had locked completely, and the faded paint along the rim of his shield flaked away whenever he set it down. Old age came for him slowly, with the dry brittleness of sun-baked polymer, until he could no longer walk the steps of the encyclopedia palace without leaning on Big Car's scarred fender.

In the quiet chambers at the foot of the dresser, they laid him upon a folded square of faded flannel. Big Car stood beside him in silence, his battered nosecone pointed toward the bed, his headlights dark. Around them waited the court—and the three brothers who carried the emblem of the dynasty: Blue Sun, Red Sun, and Gray Sun.

Captain America looked past the fiercest of them and fixed his fading gaze on Blue Sun.

Blue Sun was everything the old founder had tried to hammer into the Funky state: calm under pressure, measured in judgment, incorruptible, and genuinely virtuous. He did not covet the bombs locked behind the lower dresser drawer, nor did he speak of the Shells or the smaller baseboard towns with contempt.

"Do not mistake the weapons for the law," Captain America murmured from the flannel cloth, his voice barely louder than dust shifting in a draft. "The throne is yours, Blue Sun. Keep the room whole."

Soon after, beneath the pale afternoon bars of the blinds, the first emperor of the world died.

The succession did not survive the week.

Red Sun had watched the deathbed from the shadows of the stacked encyclopedias, and jealousy had eaten through him like acid. He saw Blue Sun's virtue not as strength but as weakness—a gentle hand unworthy of an empire forged in nuclear fire and iron union. In the dead quiet between sunset and the radiator's midnight clank, Red Sun moved through the inner chambers alone. He caught Blue Sun unguarded, murdered his own brother in secret, and buried the truth behind a hastily staged story of sudden collapse. By morning, Red Sun wore the crown of the Funcar Empire.

Big Car said little in public, bound by his oath to the union he had forged with the dead founder, yet everyone in the palace felt the cold change in the air. Under Red Sun, the Funcar Empire grew hard, suspicious, and hungry. The treasury recovered; the garrisons expanded; the hidden workshops behind the dresser kept manufacturing weapons in the dark.

Along the north wall, old Lakitu watched it all from his floating white cloud and grew quieter with every passing season. By 1998, age claimed him too. He passed away peacefully beneath the radiator, leaving the Shell Empire to a younger, fiercer successor who felt none of Lakitu's personal loyalty to the memory of Captain America—and who looked across the braided rug at Red Sun's swollen, arrogant court with open disgust.

Within months of taking power, the new Shell ruler formally broke the alliance with the Funcars.

The Betrayal of the Shells sent shockwaves across the entire floor. For eight years the west wall and the north wall had been the twin pillars of the bedroom; now their regiments clashed violently across the central wool once more. At first the Shell phalanxes fought with the fury of a people casting off a hated patron. Yet the new Shell ruler had misread his own ranks.

Too many Shells remembered 1991, when Funky soldiers had bled beside them to stop Mario, and 1994, when they had stood together against the fire of the World War. Disgusted by the broken oath and terrified of what Red Sun's arsenal would do to their homes beneath the radiator, whole companies of rebel Shells turned their backs on their new sovereign. Under cover of night they crept across the braided carpet, hailed the Funcar pickets in hushed voices, and crossed the lines to fight for the union.

Torn open from within by its own rebels, the Shell Empire collapsed. Its fortresses beneath the radiator were overrun; its independence ended in smoke and splintered paint.

Yet Red Sun did not live to enjoy his triumph. Almost immediately in the wake of the Shell betrayal—broken by the strain of the campaign, whispers of poison in his court, and the blood of Blue Sun that had never truly washed off his hands—Red Sun died.

The crown fell to the last surviving brother: Gray Sun.

Gray Sun had never been built for a throne, least of all one resting on fratricide, civil war, and stockpiled nuclear fire. Afflicted by a deep, worsening mental illness, Gray Sun walked the encyclopedia halls at all hours of the night, whispering to empty shadows, flinching violently at the metallic ticking of the radiator, convinced that the pale green plastic stars on the ceiling were shifting their formations to spy upon the palace. Orders issued at noon were reversed by dusk. Envoys waited days outside the chambers while their sovereign sat motionless, staring at the hairline cracks in the oak floorboards, unable to tell border dispatches from the ghosts of two dead brothers.

Under Gray Sun's fractured grip, the Funcar Empire did not collapse—it drifted, enormous, heavily armed, and rudderless, while generals and bureaucrats who answered to no conscience filled the vacuum around the throne.

## IV. Ash and the Moon (1999–2000)

By 1999, the Funcar Empire had no rival left on the bedroom floor.

The Mushroom Kingdom was a decade-old scorch mark. The Mario Empire lay buried behind the bookcase. The Bandits, Angels, and Japanese were dust. The Farm Empire had vanished into nothing. The Shells had been absorbed or broken. Only scattered tiny civilizations remained—fragile hamlets tucked inside the heels of worn sneakers beside the closet, behind the wicker laundry hamper, and along the quiet wooden margins near the doorway, paying tribute or praying simply not to be noticed.

Absolute power, wedded to Gray Sun's spiraling instability, curdled into madness.

There was no border incident in 1999, no coalition massing on the rug, no ultimatum delivered to the dresser steps. Yet the Funcar high command—swollen with unchecked supremacy and echoing the dread pouring from their sovereign's disordered mind—decided that the Planet itself was contaminated, treacherous, and beneath them. Without cause, provocation, or mercy, they opened the vaults behind the lower dresser drawer and loosed their nuclear stockpile across the entire bedroom floor.

Flash followed flash across the wool and oak.

The tiny civilizations along the baseboards never even knew a war had been declared before the white glare swallowed them. Sneaker-heel villages, paperclip hamlets, hidden refuges behind the laundry hamper—all were scoured to silent ash. The braided carpet turned gray and brittle; the varnished planks blistered under overlapping pale circles of ruin. A thick, dry hush settled over the room, the smell of scorched wool and ozone hanging heavy beneath the blinds.

Having destroyed the Planet for no reason at all, the Funcars looked down from the cracked steps of their palace at the wasteland they had made—and decided to abandon it.

For months, the surviving remnant of the empire prepared an exodus. They left the ruined floor behind, lashing together shoelaces, rubber bands, wooden ruler-bridges, and stacked blocks to scale the sheer vertical cliffs of the furniture beside the window. Up the hanging curtain folds they climbed, past the brass pulls and shadowed shelves, hauling their diminished court, their last supplies, and their battered pride toward the highest reachable refuge in the room: a pale, round, white-painted wooden pedestal shelf mounted high beside the upper window frame, bathed every night in cold silver moonlight and dusted in fine chalky plaster.

To the survivors who hauled themselves over its rim in the final weeks of 1999, looking down on the ruined carpet far below like a dead world, it had only one name.

The Moon.

* * *

At first, the Moon seemed a sanctuary.

The air up there was cool and still, untouched by the ash of the braided rug. Through the upper glass of the window, real night light poured over the pale round surface, throwing long, stark shadows from a heavy globe-lamp base and a leaning wooden picture frame. The Funcar exiles—all that remained of Captain America's austere beginning, Big Car's proud union, and the Three Suns' ruined dynasty—set down their packs in the chalk dust and believed they had escaped history.

They were not alone.

In the shadowed crevices behind the lamp base lived a small, forgotten organization of Junk Cars. They were the castoffs of a vanished era—rusted tin bodies, shattered windshields, stripped doors, missing tires, axles twisted beyond rolling, discarded up on the high shelf years before anyone had even laid claim to the First Lands. Down on the floor, empires had risen, signed treaties, and burned; up on the cold pale shelf, the Junk Cars had waited in silence, watching the white flashes far below and nursing a quiet, patient bitterness.

They did not attack the Funcars in the open. They were too few, and the Funcar veterans, however exhausted, were still armed.

Instead, the Junk Cars rolled and scraped slowly out from behind the picture frame with dimmed headlights and gentle voices.

"You have come a long way up the dark," their spokesman rasped through a cracked grille. "The draft off the window glass cuts sharp after midnight. Come behind the lamp base—we have intact tin, dry shelter, and room for all of you."

Exhausted, arrogant even in exile, and blind to any threat so small and broken, the Funcars followed them.

The Junk Cars led the whole remnant of the empire into a narrow, blind canyon wedged between the plaster wall, the heavy cast-metal base of the globe-lamp, and a leaning stack of old cardboard coasters. Once the last Funcar soldier stepped past the entrance, rusted chassis slammed shut across the gap like iron doors.

It was over in a single silent night in the year 2000.

Trapped in the dark with no room to form ranks, no science left to save them, and nowhere left to flee, the Funcars were cut down to the last soul. The Junk Cars took no prisoners and left no monument. When morning light finally slanted across the upper windowpane, only chipped paint, hollow plastic shards, and pale dust stirred in the draft.

The Funcar Empire—born of a knockoff's dream of order, cemented in debt, stained by fratricide, and swollen into the destroyer of a world—was dead. Nobody on the floor below even heard them fall.

## V. After the Dust (2004 and Beyond)

A bedroom floor does not stay dead forever.

Four quiet years passed after the silence on the Moon. Sunlight returned through the slats of the blinds, warming the scorched rings of the braided rug until the smell of ash finally faded into ordinary dust. Drafts from beneath the closet door swept clean corridors across the oak. Gradually, out of the deepest crevices—behind the radiator pipes, inside cracked dresser drawers, beneath the forgotten folds of winter coats in the closet bottom—survivors crept back onto the open floor.

By 2004, the Planet had recovered enough to bleed again.

Two great powers rose almost immediately from the ruins, neither resembling the rigid molded empires of the old century.

Across the soft, worn center of the braided rug rose the Plush Empire—legions of stitched cloth and matted fur, stuffed with quiet cotton that absorbed blows without cracking, moving on soft paws without a sound across the wool. Along the hard oak borders and the shadowed base of the bookcase rose the Kaiju Empire—towering, heavy-tailed vinyl giants with ridged spines, hard painted fangs, and heavy footsteps that shook the loose floorboards as they marched.

Neither would yield the floor to the other. Almost from the hour their banners went up, the Plush Empire and the Kaiju Empire plunged into a fierce revolutionary war, tearing across the recovering carpet in a clash of soft, relentless endurance against towering vinyl fury.

Yet neither empire holds the battlefield alone. Out of the old craters and forgotten graves of 1991, 1994, and 1999 has crawled a third, deeply unsettling power: the Prosthetic Empire. They are the unclaimed remnants of the vanished century—snapped-off arms, mismatched legs, spare heads, and loose joints bound together with twisted paperclips, rubber bands, and scavenged wire, walking again on borrowed sockets. Neither wholly alive in the old way nor willing to stay buried in the dust, the Prosthetic Empire harries both Plush and Kaiju flanks alike, forcing the two great rivals to fight their revolutionary war with one eye always turned toward the shadows behind them.

* * *

And beyond 2004, nothing is confirmed—only the rumors whispered along the baseboards after the bedroom lamp clicks off and the plastic ceiling stars begin their faint green glow.

Those who watch the seams of both great armies suspect that neither the Plush Empire nor the Kaiju Empire will survive the weight of their own war. Already, fractures show inside their camps—fraying stitches, hollow boasts, exhausted supply lines. If both empires collapse from within, as many along the oak border now whisper they will, the floor will not fall silent. Seven new nations wait in the margins to carve up the room.

Near the bright strip of morning light, observers already speak of the Tylonian Republic, led by Purrate—an intelligent, sharp-eyed, and widely loved prime minister who has somehow won genuine trust in a world that remembers too many betrayals. Against that rare hope stand four engines of raw tyranny gathering along the darker walls: the Monster Empire, crushed beneath the howling, relentless oppression of Tazmania; the Nickelodeon Empire, ruled by Ninja Turtle, a merciless autocrat whose sprawling economy runs openly on slavery; the Spider Empire, locked under the suffocating, extreme oppression of Spiderman; and the Tsardom of Nuks, where Charmeleon governs through terror and extreme oppression, his painted flame throwing long, cruel shadows across the wood.

Stranger than all of them is the looming bulk of the Alaskan Empire, sprawling across an astonishing expanse of the cold floorboards beneath the window—one of the largest empires in the entire history of the bedroom, somehow ruled by Super Teddy, an indifferent, unintelligent bear who scarcely seems to grasp the borders of his own dominion even as dust-motes settle undisturbed across his crown.

And off to one quiet corner, neat, angular, and deliberately apart from the clamor, stands Minecraft United, guided by Creeper—a silent, neutral figure presiding over a rare, stable nation that keeps its edges sharp and its treaties simple.

None of it is confirmed yet. The revolutionary war still rages across the braided wool; the Prosthetic legions still rattle along the baseboards; high overhead on the pale round shelf of the Moon, the Junk Cars remain silent in the chalk dust.

Yet down on the oak planks, as the horizontal bars of daylight creep slowly toward the bedskirt—where, in the dark behind the plaid hem, a small red T-Rex still waits motionless among the tangled springs—every watcher who remembers 1987 knows the truth of the room. Whether the seven rumored nations rise next spring or the spring after, the peace will not hold. Sooner or later, beneath the faint green plaster stars, another World War will come.
